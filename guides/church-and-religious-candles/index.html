What makes church and religious candles special?

By Steven · Updated 22 November 2026

  • The Catholic Church and Orthodox churches have long preferred beeswax for candles in worship, and Catholic altar candles must still be made of wax.
  • Different candles have different jobs, from the tall Paschal candle lit at Easter to the small votives people light with a prayer.
  • Candles and lamps matter in many faiths, including Shabbat and Hanukkah lights, Diwali diyas and lamps offered in Buddhist temples.
  • Candles for worship are made by specialist suppliers that churches approve and buy from. They aren't something to make at home, collect or recycle.

Walk into almost any old church and you’ll smell it before you see it: a mix of warm beeswax, old stone and a little incense. Candles have been part of worship for a very long time, and the ones made for churches are a little different from the candles most of us buy for home. This is a short guide to what makes them special, and to candles and lamps in some other faiths. It’s background for the curious rather than religious instruction, and customs vary a lot between churches, communities and families.

Why beeswax?

Before paraffin was refined from oil in the 1800s, the main choices for candles were beeswax and tallow, which is rendered animal fat. Tallow was cheap, but it smoked, smelled and dripped. Beeswax burned brighter and cleaner, smelled sweet and was expensive, so it was the natural choice for the altar.

Beeswax also carries meaning. In Catholic tradition the wax made by bees has long been seen as a symbol of purity. In one well-known explanation, which the United States Conference of Catholic Bishops still quotes, the wax stands for the body of Christ, the wick for his soul and the flame for his divinity.

What the rules say. For a long time, Catholic altar candles had to be made largely of beeswax, with the exact percentage left to the local bishop. Church suppliers still sell candles by their beeswax content, and you’ll often see figures such as 51% or 25% beeswax in their catalogues. Today the general rules of the Catholic Mass don’t set a percentage. In the United States, the bishops’ guidance is that altar candles must still be made of wax, and that electric lights aren’t a substitute. The Paschal candle is traditionally expected to be made mostly of beeswax. Other churches, including the Orthodox churches, also strongly prefer beeswax, but there’s no single rule that covers every church, so it’s best described as tradition and guidance.

Candles you’ll see in a church

Altar candles are the tall candles standing on or near the altar, usually in pairs. Many are fitted with a small brass cap called a follower, which sits on top of the candle and stops the wax running down the sides as it burns.

The Paschal candle is the large candle lit at Easter. In Catholic, Anglican and some other churches, a new one is blessed and lit each year at the Easter Vigil on Holy Saturday night, from a fire lit outside the church. It’s marked with a cross, the Greek letters alpha and omega, and the numbers of the current year, and five grains of incense are pressed into it to represent the five wounds of Christ. It stands near the altar through the Easter season, and for the rest of the year it’s usually kept by the font and lit for baptisms and funerals.

Votive and prayer candles are the small candles that people light themselves, often while saying a prayer for someone. The word comes from the Latin votum, meaning a vow or promise. They usually stand on a rack or in a tray of sand, and there’s often a box for a donation.

The sanctuary lamp is a light that burns continuously near the tabernacle in Catholic churches, and in some Anglican churches, to show that the consecrated bread is kept there. It’s often a candle or oil lamp in red glass.

Candlemas, on 2 February, is the feast when many churches bless the candles they’ll use through the year.

In Orthodox churches, worshippers often buy thin beeswax tapers as they come in and light them in front of icons, standing them upright in stands filled with sand.

Advent and Christmas

The Advent wreath is a ring of greenery with four candles, one lit on each Sunday of Advent, and sometimes a fifth in the middle for Christmas Day. The modern version is usually credited to Johann Hinrich Wichern, a Lutheran pastor who in 1839 hung a cartwheel with candles in the children’s home he ran in Hamburg. Colours vary. Many churches use purple or blue candles with a pink one for the third Sunday, and in Britain four red candles are common. Some families use white or whatever they have.

The Christingle is an orange with a candle pushed into the top, a red ribbon round the middle, and cocktail sticks of dried fruit or sweets. It began in the Moravian Church in 1747. In 1968 John Pensom of The Children’s Society brought Christingle services to the Church of England as a fundraiser, and they’ve been a fixture of British Advent and Christmas ever since. The orange stands for the world, the candle for Christ as the light of the world, the ribbon for his blood, and the four sticks for the fruits of the earth and the four seasons.

Jewish candles

Shabbat candles are lit on Friday evening before sunset to welcome the Sabbath. Most households light at least two, and many follow the custom of lighting them about 18 minutes before sunset.

The Havdalah candle, lit when the Sabbath ends on Saturday evening, is a braided candle with several wicks, so it burns with one larger flame. It’s one of the oldest multi-wick candles still in everyday use.

Hanukkah, the festival of lights, lasts eight nights. Candles are lit in a hanukkiah, a candle holder with nine branches: eight for the nights and one for the shamash, the helper candle used to light the others. One more candle is added each night.

Yahrzeit candles are lit before sundown on the anniversary of a death and burn for around 24 hours. They’re usually sold in a small glass or tin.

Diwali and other faiths

Diwali, celebrated by Hindus, Sikhs, Jains and some Buddhists, is known as the festival of lights. Homes are lit with diyas, small clay lamps filled with ghee or oil, with a cotton wick. For Sikhs, the same time of year is also Bandi Chhor Divas, when the Golden Temple at Amritsar is lit up. Many families now use candles and electric lights alongside traditional diyas.

In Buddhist practice, candles and lamps are common offerings at shrines and temples, and butter lamps are part of Tibetan Buddhist tradition. Light is often linked with wisdom and understanding.

Many other faiths and cultures use candles too, from remembrance and memorial candles to candles lit for festivals and family occasions. The common thread is light as a sign of hope, presence and remembering.

How church candles are made

Church candles are made to burn slowly, steadily and cleanly, often for hours at a time in a draughty building.

  • Dipping and pouring. The traditional way is to dip wicks into melted wax again and again, building up the candle layer by layer, or to pour wax over hanging wicks. Many beeswax tapers are still made like this.
  • Extruding. Many modern altar and pillar candles are made by forcing wax through a die in a long length and cutting it to size, which gives very even, dense candles.
  • Dense, hard wax. Beeswax has a high melting point, so it burns slowly and drips less. Church candles often mix beeswax with stearin or paraffin to control cost and hardness.
  • Matched wicks. The wick is sized so the flame uses up the wax at the right rate, with little smoke and no hollowing out.
  • Oil candles. Some churches now use refillable tubes filled with liquid wax or lamp oil, which look like candles from a distance and never drip.

Made by approved suppliers

Candles for use in worship are made by specialist church candle makers that churches approve and buy from, to the church’s own requirements. Many are blessed before they’re used, and churches have their own respectful ways of dealing with what’s left once they’ve burned down. They aren’t something to copy at home, and it isn’t appropriate to try to get hold of, buy up or melt down candles that have been used in a church, such as votive ends or an old Paschal candle. If you’d like to light a candle in a church, do it the way the church offers, which is usually a votive by the door with a box for a donation.

Candles at home

Plenty of people like a plain beeswax candle at home, for a quiet corner, a moment of remembrance or simply for the honey smell. That’s a home candle rather than a church candle, and if you give or sell one, it’s worth being clear about the difference. Don’t describe a candle you’ve made as an altar, Paschal or blessed candle.
