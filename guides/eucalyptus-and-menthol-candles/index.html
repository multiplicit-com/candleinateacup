Eucalyptus, mint and menthol candles: fresh winter scents made safely

By Steven · Updated 22 November 2026

  • Eucalyptus, peppermint and menthol make lovely fresh winter scents, but they're candles, not remedies, so don't describe them as treating anything.
  • Some of these oils can be harmful to babies, young children, cats and dogs, and strong scents can set off asthma.
  • Use a candle fragrance oil with a eucalyptus or mint profile rather than menthol crystals, and keep the amount low.
  • Burn them only in a well-aired room, and never near a sleeping child or where pets can't get away from the scent.

When the weather turns cold and damp, a lot of people reach for fresh, clean scents: eucalyptus, peppermint, a hint of menthol, perhaps with lemon or pine. They smell like a walk on a frosty morning and make a nice change from the sweet scents of Christmas. You can make them in a teacup like any other scented candle, but these particular ingredients need more care than most, so read the safety section before you start.

Candles, not remedies

It’s tempting to call these “cold and flu” or “breathe easy” candles, but please don’t. A candle isn’t a medicine, and there’s no good evidence that a scented candle treats or relieves anything. In the UK, only licensed medicines are allowed to claim they treat or ease an illness. If you sell candles, describing them that way breaks the advertising rules and could mislead someone who’s unwell.

Describe the scent instead: fresh, clean, cooling, crisp, “clear air”, winter woodland. If anyone is unwell, a pharmacist is the right person to ask.

Safety first

These ingredients can cause real harm in the wrong situation.

  • Babies and young children. Strong menthol, eucalyptus and peppermint scents can affect a young child’s breathing. Don’t burn these candles in a baby’s or young child’s room, and never leave any candle burning near a sleeping child. Eucalyptus and other essential oils can be dangerous if swallowed, so keep the bottles well out of reach.
  • Pets. Cats are especially sensitive to essential oils, including eucalyptus, peppermint and tea tree, and dogs can be affected too. Keep the candle out of reach and make sure pets can leave the room. Don’t burn these scents in a small room where a pet sleeps.
  • Asthma and allergies. Strong scents can set off asthma, headaches and wheezing. Keep the scent light, and if anyone in the house has asthma, leave these candles out or ask them first.
  • Ventilation. Burn them in a well-aired room, not a small closed room for hours, and stop if anyone feels unwell.
  • Pregnancy. Some people prefer to avoid strong essential oils during pregnancy. If in doubt, ask a midwife or pharmacist.

The usual candle rules apply too. See our safety guide.

Which oils to use

Candle fragrance oils with a eucalyptus, mint or “clear air” profile are the easiest and most predictable choice. They’re made to cope with hot wax and come with a safety data sheet listing the flash point and the allergens they contain. Many blend eucalyptus with softer notes like lavender, lemon or cedar, which makes for a rounder, less medicinal scent.

Essential oils such as eucalyptus and peppermint can be used, but only buy ones sold for candle making, and treat them with care. Some have a lower flash point than typical fragrance oils, which means their vapour can catch light at a lower temperature. Add them at the lowest temperature your wax supplier allows, never overheat the wax, and never leave it unattended. Our guide to scenting teacup candles compares fragrance oils and essential oils in more detail.

Menthol crystals are sold for some home remedies and are sometimes added to candles. We wouldn’t. They’re very strong, they’re easy to overdo, and you can’t easily measure a safe amount for a small teacup. A fragrance oil with a menthol note gives you the cooling smell without the guesswork.

How much to use

Keep it low. These scents are strong, and a teacup is small. Even if your wax will take 10% fragrance, aim well below that, and let eucalyptus or mint be only part of the blend rather than all of it. Mixing it with lemon, lavender or a soft wood makes it pleasant at a lower strength. You can always make the next one stronger.

Our how much wax calculator works out the grams of wax and fragrance for any percentage.

Make it the same way as any scented candle: melt the wax gently in a water bath, add the fragrance at the right temperature, stir for two minutes, pour, and let it cure for a week or two. The instructable has every step.

Scent ideas

  • Eucalyptus and lemon, bright and clean.
  • Eucalyptus, mint and lavender, softer and more relaxing.
  • Peppermint and cedar, a crisp winter woodland.
  • Pine, eucalyptus and frost, for December.
  • Mint tea with spearmint and green tea, which suits a teacup particularly well.

A white or pale green wax in a cup with a green or blue pattern looks fresh and wintry. See our colour palettes for ideas.

Without a flame

If you just want the smell, there are simpler ways that don’t need a candle. An adult can put a drop of eucalyptus oil on a tissue, kept well away from children and pets, or enjoy the scent of a eucalyptus bunch hung in a steamy bathroom. An unscented teacup candle, or an LED one, is a nice thing to sit beside on a winter evening too.
