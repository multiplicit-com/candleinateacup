A wax melt warmer gently heats a small piece of scented wax so it melts and fills the room with scent. They’re cheap, they come in every style imaginable, and they’re a good way to use up leftover candle wax. Our guide to melting down old candles shows how to clean old wax up first. This guide covers the different kinds of warmer, which we’d pick, and what to check before you buy.

How we chose

We haven’t tested every warmer on the market, and the models on sale change all the time, so we’ve picked by type rather than by brand. We looked at how each kind heats the wax, how safe it is to leave running in a room you’re in, how easy it is to change the wax, and what can go wrong. The links below take you to warmers of each type so you can compare current models and reviews.

What to look for.

  • A timer or automatic shut-off. It’s the most useful safety feature a warmer can have.
  • A removable dish that’s easy to clean.
  • A stable base that won’t tip if it’s knocked.
  • A UK plug, made for UK mains, with a UKCA or CE mark. Be wary of cheap imports with no markings or with an adaptor in the box.
  • If it uses a bulb, check replacement bulbs are easy to buy.

Good alternatives

Plug-in warmers. Small warmers that plug straight into a wall socket. They’re tidy and good for a hallway, landing or bathroom, but they hold only a little wax, and you need to make sure the plug is the right way up so the wax doesn’t spill.

Candle warmer lamps. A lamp on a stand shines down onto the top of a candle and melts the surface without lighting the wick. They’re made for jar candles but work just as well on a teacup candle, and they’re popular where open flames aren’t allowed or wanted. The candle lasts much longer, as no wax is burned away, though the scent fades over time. The bulbs get very hot, so check the lamp height suits your cup and keep it away from anything that could be knocked into it.

Warming plates. A flat heated plate that a candle stands on. They work best with candles in thin glass jars. China cups are thicker and warm more slowly, so a plate is less effective under a teacup than a lamp.

Using any warmer safely

  • Put it on a flat, heat-proof surface, away from curtains, shelves and anything that can catch.
  • Never add water to the dish.
  • Don’t move a warmer while the wax is liquid. Let it cool and set first.
  • To change the scent, let the wax set, then pop it out. A few minutes in the freezer helps, if the dish can go in the freezer.
  • Switch electric warmers off at the wall when you go out or to bed, even if they have a timer.
  • One or two small melts is usually plenty for an average room. More wax doesn’t give much more scent, and it makes spills more likely.

Can I use a teacup as the dish?

We wouldn’t put a teacup directly over a tealight. Old china can crack with uneven heat, and gold rims and decoration can be damaged. A china saucer on top of a purpose-made burner frame is also a risk for the same reason. Keep your cups for candles, and use a warmer that’s made for the job. If you like the look of china, plenty of warmers are made with ceramic dishes in floral patterns.
