Multi-wick candles: when a cup or bowl needs two or three wicks

By Steven · Updated 22 November 2026

Once you’ve made a few teacup candles, the bigger pieces of a tea set start to look tempting. Sugar bowls, slop bowls, breakfast cups and soup coupes all make lovely candles. The catch is that a single wick can only melt so far across. Past a certain width you either get a ring of hard wax left round the edge, or you put in a wick so big it smokes. The answer is two or three smaller wicks.

How wide before you need another wick?

There’s no exact cut-off, because it depends on the wax and the wicks you use. These are the widths we’d start testing at, measured across the inside of the vessel at the level the wax will come to:

Inside width Starting point
Up to about 9cm One wick
About 9.5cm to 13cm Two wicks
Over about 13cm Three wicks

Soy, rapeseed and beeswax are slower to melt to the edge, so you may need the extra wick at the lower end of each range. Coconut blends and paraffin melt more easily and may manage with one wick a little wider. Our wick size calculator suggests one, two or three wicks from the width.

Why most teacups only need one

An ordinary teacup measures roughly 7cm to 9cm across inside, and that’s well within what a single wick can melt. Two wicks in a teacup would sit very close together. Their flames heat each other, burn hotter and taller than either would on its own, and heat the thin china more than it needs. So unless you’re using a very wide, shallow cup, stick to one wick. Our guide to wax and wicks covers sizing a single wick.

Choosing the wicks

Use the same type of wick for all of them, from the same series and the same size, so they burn evenly. Each wick only needs to melt its own patch, not the whole width, so it should be smaller than a single wick for that width would be.

A good place to start is a wick sold for containers about half to two-thirds of the full width. For a sugar bowl 11cm across, that means trying wicks sold for 6cm to 7cm containers. Then do a test burn, and go up or down a size from there. As with single wicks, the wax, fragrance and dye all make a difference, so a supplier’s chart is only a starting point.

Where to put them

  • Two wicks: on a straight line through the middle, each about a quarter of the width in from the side. In an 11cm bowl, that’s roughly 2.5cm to 3cm in from each edge.
  • Three wicks: in a triangle, evenly spaced, each about halfway between the centre and the side.
  • Not too near the sides. A flame close to the china heats one spot and can crack it. Keep each wick at least 2cm from the side.
  • Not too close together. Flames a couple of centimetres apart pull towards each other and burn hotter. If your wicks end up crowded, the vessel probably doesn’t need that many.

Stick each wick down with its own wick sticker before you pour, and hold them upright with a centring bar that has two or three holes. Clothes pegs work for two wicks, but a bar is much easier for three.

Keeping the melt pool even

  • Light all of them, every time. If you only light one, it tunnels down on its own and the others are left high and dry.
  • Trim them all to the same length, about 5mm, before each burn. A longer wick burns hotter and melts its side faster.
  • Give the first burn time to reach the edge. As with any candle, let the melted wax spread to the edge on the first burn so it doesn’t tunnel later. In a wide bowl this can take three to four hours. Don’t go past four hours.
  • Keep it out of draughts, which push the flames to one side and melt the wax unevenly.

Heat and the risk of cracking

Two or three flames put out much more heat than one, and china is less forgiving than the thick glass jars sold for candles. A few things lower the risk:

  • Use thick, sound pieces. Check bowls for hairline cracks by holding them up to the light, and run a finger round the rim for chips. Leave fine, eggshell-thin china and anything that’s been glued or riveted for single wicks or LED lights.
  • Fine crazing in the glaze is usually fine, but a crack through the body of the piece isn’t. Our teacup preparation guide explains how to tell the difference.
  • Stop with about 1cm of wax left. As the wax gets low, the flames are close to the base and it gets hot.
  • Stand it on something heatproof. A wide candle gets warm underneath. A plate, tile or trivet protects the table. See saucer or no saucer for ideas.

Teapots

Don’t pour wax straight into a china teapot and give it several wicks. The opening is narrow compared with the body, so the heat stays trapped inside, and a teapot is very hard to clean out afterwards. For a teapot, the safer approach is a separate candle insert that sits inside it. Our glass teapot candle project shows how.

Working out the wax

Multi-wick candles take a lot of wax, so measure the vessel with water first.
