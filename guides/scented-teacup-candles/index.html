How to scent teacup candles: fragrance oils, essential oils and how much to add

A teacup candle doesn’t have to be scented. An unscented candle in a pretty cup is lovely, and it’s the kindest choice for anyone with allergies or asthma. But a good scent is what most people want, and it’s easy to get right once you know a few rules. This guide covers the oils, the amounts, the timing and some scents that suit a teacup.

Fragrance oils or essential oils?

Fragrance oils are blends made for candles and soaps. Some are made from synthetic ingredients, some include natural ones, and many are a mix. Those sold for candles have been made to cope with hot wax and to give off scent when burned. They’re the easiest choice by far, and they come in thousands of scents, including ones like Earl Grey or English pear that you couldn’t get from a plant.

Essential oils are oils extracted from plants, such as lavender or eucalyptus. They can work in candles, but there are drawbacks:

  • They’re often weak in wax. Many essential oils are quite volatile, which means they evaporate easily, so their scent can fade in the hot wax or in the finished candle.
  • They cost more. You need quite a lot for a teacup candle, and the price adds up fast.
  • “Natural” doesn’t mean safer. Essential oils are concentrated chemicals like any other. Some can irritate skin, some are harmful to pets, and most contain the same allergens that fragrance oils are labelled for. If you sell candles, essential oils need hazard labelling too, and often more of it.

If you like the idea of a natural scent, look for fragrance oils described as containing essential oils, or buy essential oils sold specifically for candle making from a candle supplier. Don’t use the little bottles meant for oil burners or the bath.

How much fragrance?

Fragrance is measured by weight as a percentage of the whole candle. This is called the fragrance load. Most container waxes take about 6% to 10%, and many suppliers give a maximum of 10% or 12%. Your wax supplier will tell you the maximum for their wax, and you should never go over it. Too much oil can seep out of the wax, make the candle smoke and in the worst case make the surface catch light.

  • Soy and rapeseed: usually 6% to 10%. Start at 8%.
  • Coconut blends: often take a little more, so check the supplier’s figure.
  • Paraffin: usually 6% to 10%, and it gives off scent strongly.
  • Beeswax: doesn’t hold added fragrance well, and it has its own honey smell. Most people leave it unscented or use a low amount.

More isn’t always stronger. Past a certain point the wax can’t hold any more, and the scent doesn’t improve. A good wick and a proper cure make more difference.

How much is that in grams? The fragrance counts as part of the total weight. For a cup that takes 172g of wax in total, 8% is about 14g of fragrance and 158g of wax. Our how much wax calculator works it out for any cup and any percentage.

When to add it

Melt the wax in a jug standing in a pan of simmering water, then take it off the heat when it reaches the temperature your supplier gives for adding fragrance. For many soy waxes this is around 80°C to 85°C. Pour in the fragrance and stir slowly and steadily for a full two minutes, so it binds with the wax rather than sitting in pockets. Then let it cool to your pouring temperature.

Don’t add fragrance to wax that’s much hotter than the supplier says, and never heat it directly on the hob. Every fragrance oil has a flash point, the temperature at which its vapour could catch light near a flame. You won’t reach it in a water bath at normal candle temperatures, but it’s one reason to melt wax gently and never leave it unattended. The flash point is printed on the oil’s safety data sheet.

Curing

A scented candle smells much stronger if you leave it to rest before lighting. Soy and rapeseed candles are usually best after one to two weeks. Paraffin needs less time, often a few days. Keep curing candles somewhere cool and out of direct sunlight, with a lid or a sheet of paper over the top to keep dust off.

Hot throw and cold throw

Cold throw is how the candle smells when it isn’t lit, for example when someone sniffs it at a craft fair. Hot throw is how well the scent fills the room once it’s burning. A candle can be good at one and weak at the other. Hot throw depends a lot on the melt pool. A wick that’s too small never melts enough wax to release the scent, so if a candle smells lovely in the cup but of nothing when lit, check the wick before adding more fragrance. Our wax and wicks guide explains how.

Teacups are small, so even a good candle will scent a small room better than a large open-plan one.

Scents that suit a teacup

The tea-room classics are popular for a reason:

  • Earl Grey, with bergamot and black tea. The obvious one, and lovely in a blue-and-white cup.
  • Rose in a cup with roses on it. It never gets old.
  • Lavender, often paired with white tea or vanilla.
  • Vanilla, cream and honey for something warm and soft.
  • English pear and freesia, fig, or honeysuckle for a lighter, fresh scent.
  • Lemon drizzle, Victoria sponge or scones and jam if you want to go full afternoon tea.
  • Spiced orange, cinnamon and clove for winter. See our Christmas guide.

Matching the scent to the pattern on the cup is a nice touch, and so is matching the wax colour. Our colour palettes guide has ideas.

Vanilla turns wax brown

Many vanilla scents contain vanillin, which slowly turns wax yellow, then tan or brown, over days or weeks. It doesn’t affect how the candle burns, but it can spoil a white or pastel candle. The safety data sheet usually lists how much vanillin is in a fragrance. To avoid surprises, use vanilla scents in cream, caramel or brown wax where a little darkening won’t show, or look for a vanilla fragrance sold as non-discolouring. Our guide to colouring wax covers choosing colours.

Safety

  • Ventilate. Burn scented candles in a room with some fresh air, and not in a small closed room for hours.
  • Allergies and asthma. Strong scents can trigger asthma and headaches in some people. If you’re making a gift, a light scent or none at all is the safer choice.
  • Pets. Cats and dogs are more sensitive to scent than we are, and some essential oils, including tea tree, eucalyptus and peppermint, can be harmful to them. Keep candles where pets can’t reach, and make sure they can leave the room.
  • Store oils safely. Keep fragrance and essential oils out of reach of children, as they can be harmful if swallowed.
  • Labelling if you sell. Scented candles sold in Great Britain usually need hazard labelling under the CLP Regulation, based on the fragrance supplier’s safety data sheet. Our guide to trade suppliers explains more.
