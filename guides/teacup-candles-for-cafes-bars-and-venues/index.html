Teacup candles for cafes, bars, tea rooms and venues

By Steven · Updated 22 November 2026

We may earn a commission from links on this page. How affiliate links work

  • Teacup candles suit tea rooms, cafes, bars, B&Bs and wedding tables, and the cups can be reused once the wax has gone.
  • A real flame in a business comes under fire safety law. Include candles in your fire risk assessment and check your insurance.
  • Choose low, wide cups with saucers, keep them away from anything that can catch fire, and make one person responsible for lighting and putting them out.
  • LED teacup candles give much the same look with no flame, and are the easy choice for busy or unsupervised spaces.

A teacup candle on each table is an easy way to make a tea room, a cafe or a small bar feel warm and a bit special, especially on winter evenings. They suit wedding tables and B&B breakfast rooms too, and a few on a shop counter or in a window look lovely. This guide covers the ideas that work, the safety side that a business needs to take seriously, the practical bits that make them easier to run during a busy service, and when an LED version is the better choice.

We’re not lawyers or fire safety advisers. Where this guide mentions the law, it’s to point you to the official guidance, which you should read for your own premises.

Where they work well

  • Tea rooms and cafes. The obvious fit. Mismatched vintage cups on each table suit a vintage-style room, and the cups can match the ones you serve in.
  • Bars and restaurants. A darker, single-colour set, such as black and gold cups with deep red or black wax, suits evening service better than pastel florals. See our black teacup candles guide for ideas.
  • B&Bs and guest houses. In the breakfast room or lounge, where staff are around. Not in bedrooms, where nobody is watching them.
  • Wedding and event venues. Teacup candles make good table centres and can double as favours for guests to take home.
  • Shops. A few lit on a counter or in a display can help sell teacup candles, china or gifts, as long as they’re well away from stock.

Fire safety and your responsibilities

This is the part to read carefully. A candle in your own home is your own business. A candle in a business open to the public brings legal responsibilities.

In England and Wales, the Regulatory Reform (Fire Safety) Order 2005 requires the “responsible person” for non-domestic premises, usually the owner, employer or whoever controls the premises, to carry out a fire risk assessment and act on it. Scotland and Northern Ireland have their own equivalent laws with similar duties. Open flames such as candles are an ignition risk, so if you use them they belong in your fire risk assessment, along with how you’ll control the risk.

The official guidance on GOV.UK, and your local fire and rescue service, are the places to start. Many fire services offer free advice to small businesses.

It’s also worth checking:

  • Your insurance. Some policies have conditions about naked flames, or need to know you use them. Ask your insurer before you start.
  • Your landlord or venue. Leases, and venues you hire, sometimes ban or restrict candles. Wedding venues in particular often have their own rules, and many only allow enclosed flames or LEDs.
  • Licensing conditions. If you have a premises licence, check it doesn’t say anything about naked flames.

Practical safety for busy tables

These are sensible habits rather than legal advice, and your own risk assessment comes first.

  • Choose stable cups. Low, wide cups with a saucer are much harder to knock over than tall, narrow ones. Avoid cups with wobbly bases.
  • Keep the flame low in the cup. A candle that has burned down a little, or a tealight sitting low on sand, keeps the flame below the rim and away from sleeves and menus.
  • Mind what’s nearby. Keep candles away from curtains, blinds, paper decorations, flowers, menus and coat hooks, and out of walkways where people brush past.
  • Never leave them unattended. Make putting candles out part of closing down each area, and make one named person responsible for it on each shift.
  • Light and put out with the right tools. A long lighter for lighting and a snuffer for putting out are quicker and safer than matches and blowing.
  • Check them during service. Long wicks, smoking flames and candles burned too low all need dealing with. Trim wicks to about 5mm before each shift.
  • Know when to retire one. Stop using a candle when there’s about 1cm of wax left, and take any cracked cup out of use straight away.

Our safety and care guide has more on looking after teacup candles. For a venue, the basics are the same but they need to be written down and followed every shift.

Burn time and refilling

An average teacup candle will usually give somewhere in the region of 25 to 40 hours, though that depends on the size of the cup, the wax and the wick. If you light them for a four-hour evening service, that’s roughly a couple of weeks of evenings each. Don’t burn them for much longer than four hours at a stretch.

A tealight in a teacup lasts about four hours, or longer for long-burning ones, which suits a single service. Swapping tealights is quick, they’re cheap to buy in bulk, and a layer of sand in the cup keeps them level and the heat away from the china. Our tealight holders guide explains how.

Keep a box of spare candles or cups, so a finished or chipped one can be swapped without fuss. When a poured candle runs out, the cup can be cleaned and refilled. See caring for old china for getting the old wax out.

Scent

Go easy on scent in a cafe or restaurant. Strong fragrances compete with the smell of the food and coffee, and some customers find them unpleasant or bad for asthma and allergies. Unscented candles are the safer choice where people are eating and drinking. A light scent can work in a shop or a hotel lounge.

LED teacup candles

For many venues an LED teacup candle is simply the better option. There’s no flame, so there’s nothing to supervise, nothing to knock over and nothing for the insurer to worry about. You can put them in bedrooms, near curtains, on high shelves and in children’s areas, and you can leave them on.

The better ones flicker convincingly, and in a cup you can’t see much of the plastic. Look for sets with timers, so they come on and go off by themselves, or rechargeable ones with a charging base if you have a lot of them, as changing button batteries on thirty tables gets old quickly.

A mix works well in many places: real candles on tables where staff are always nearby, and LEDs everywhere else.

Buying in bulk from makers

If you want real teacup candles in quantity, small makers are often happy to talk about trade or bulk orders, and some will match the cups or wax to your colours or theme. A few things to agree up front:

  • How many, and how quickly they can make them, as each cup is a one-off
  • Whether the cups will match, or are deliberately mixed
  • The scent, or whether they can be unscented
  • Wax type, and the burn time they expect for the size of cup
  • Whether they’ll refill your cups when the candles run out, which saves buying new ones
