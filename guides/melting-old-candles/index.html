Melting down old candles to make teacup candles

By Steven · Updated 22 November 2026

Most people have a few candles that have burned down unevenly or that they’ve stopped using, and the wax left in them is perfectly reusable. A teacup is a good home for it. You don’t need much wax to fill one, and an odd-coloured candle looks fine in a patterned cup.

Safety first

Wax is flammable, and old candles can hide surprises, so take a bit more care than you would with fresh wax.

  • Always melt wax in a jug standing in a pan of simmering water. Never put it straight on the hob, where it can overheat and catch fire.
  • Don’t melt old candles in the microwave. Many have metal wick tabs at the bottom, and wax heats unevenly in a microwave.
  • Keep a thermometer in the wax and don’t let it smoke. If it starts smoking, take it off the heat.
  • If wax ever catches fire, turn off the heat and smother it with a lid or a damp tea towel. Never use water.
  • Stay in the kitchen the whole time the wax is on the heat.

Sorting what you’ve got

Before you melt anything, sort the candle ends into groups. A bit of sorting now avoids a jug of muddy grey-brown wax later.

By colour. Mixing lots of colours tends to give a dull brown or grey. Keep whites and creams together, keep similar shades together, and save darker colours for a darker candle.

By scent. Leftover fragrances can clash, and three different scents in one candle rarely smell good. Unscented and lightly scented ends are the most useful.

By wax type, if you know it. Different waxes can be melted together, but the result is less predictable. A mix of soy and paraffin, for example, may frost, set unevenly or need a different wick from either wax on its own. If you know which candles were soy and which were paraffin, it’s worth keeping them apart. Our wax types guide explains how they differ.

Leave out candles with glitter, dried flowers or anything embedded in the wax. Those bits can catch light or clog the new wick.

Getting the wax out

From jars and tins: put the container in the freezer for a few hours. The wax shrinks as it gets cold and usually comes out in one piece with a nudge from a butter knife. For soft wax that won’t pop out, stand the jar in a bowl of hot water for a few minutes until the edges loosen.

From pillars and tapers: break or cut them into small pieces so they melt evenly.

From tealights: push the wax out of the aluminium cup from underneath.

Pull out the old wicks and metal tabs and throw them away. Old wicks are coated in soot and burn badly, so always use a new one.

Melting and cleaning the wax

Put the pieces in a metal pouring jug and stand it in a saucepan with a few centimetres of simmering water. Stir now and then as it melts. Once it’s fully liquid, pour it slowly through a fine metal strainer, or a piece of old tights stretched over a second jug, to catch soot, bits of wick and any debris. You’ll be surprised how much comes out.

Adding colour or scent

Old scented wax often smells weaker than it did when new, because some of the fragrance has burned off or faded. You can add fresh fragrance oil, but go easy. You don’t know how much fragrance is already in the wax, and too much can make it sweat or burn badly. Around 5% of fresh oil is a sensible amount to try.

If the colour has come out muddy, a few drops of a darker dye can rescue it. Our guide to colouring candle wax covers which dyes to use.

Pouring into a teacup

From here it’s the same as making a candle from new wax. Warm the cup, fix a new wick in the centre, let the wax cool to around 60°C and pour. Our teacup candle instructable goes through each step with diagrams.

Because you don’t know exactly what’s in recycled wax, the wick size is more of a guess than usual. Do a test burn of three to four hours and adjust next time. If the melt pool hasn’t reached the edge, go up a size. If the flame is tall or smoky, go down.
