This is a bigger project than our teacup candle instructable, though not a harder one. A glass teapot with a candle glowing inside makes a lovely lantern for a table or a mantelpiece, and you can see the flame from every side.

The trick is that the candle never touches the teapot. It sits in its own small tin or glass votive on a bed of sand, so when it burns down you lift it out and drop in a new one. The teapot stays clean, and you don’t have to scrape old wax out of the glass.

Time30 minutes, plus a day if you pour your own insert
Makes1 teapot lantern

Why not just pour wax into the teapot

It’s tempting, but please don’t. Even heat-resistant glass doesn’t like being hot in one place and cool in another, which is exactly what a flame burning down into a pot of wax does. The glass near the flame gets much hotter than the rest, and that difference is what cracks glass, sometimes with hot wax inside it. Thin glass teapots are the worst for this. A candle that’s poured into its own container avoids the problem, and it’s far easier to clean.

Materials

  • A heat-resistant glass teapot, usually sold as borosilicate glass. A plain round pot holding 600ml to 1 litre is a good size.
  • A small candle tin or a thick-walled glass votive that fits through the lid opening
  • A tealight, or a candle poured into the tin
  • Clean, dry sand, about a mugful

Tools

  • A ruler or tape measure
  • A long lighter or long matches
  • A spoon or small funnel for the sand

Step 1: Choose the teapot and measure the opening

Look for a teapot sold as heat-resistant or borosilicate glass. These are made to take boiling water and cope with heat far better than ordinary glass. Check it carefully for chips and cracks, especially round the base. If it came with a glass or metal infuser basket, take that out and keep it somewhere safe.

Then measure across the lid opening. This is the number that decides what insert you can use, and on some teapots it’s surprisingly narrow.

Tip: Wide, squat teapots work better than tall, narrow ones. There's more room round the flame and the heat has an easier way out.

Step 2: Choose or make the insert

The insert needs to go through the opening with at least 1cm to spare, so you can get your fingers or a pair of tongs round it. There are two easy options.

A tealight in a glass votive. This is the simplest. The votive lifts the tealight off the sand and the glass walls catch the light nicely. When the tealight is finished you swap it for a new one.

A candle poured into a tin. This burns for much longer. Pour it exactly as you would a teacup candle, following our instructable, but use a small metal tin instead of a cup and a wick sized for the tin. Keep it small. A tin around 5cm to 6cm across with a single small wick is plenty inside a teapot.

Tip: Make two or three tins at once so you always have a spare ready to drop in.

Step 3: Add a layer of sand

Spoon 2cm to 3cm of clean, dry sand into the teapot and shake it level. The sand gives the insert something firm to sit in, keeps it upright and spreads the heat from the bottom of the tin so it doesn’t all go into one spot on the glass.

A funnel made from rolled paper makes this tidier if the opening is small.

Step 4: Centre the insert

Lower the insert in and press it gently into the sand right in the middle of the pot. You want at least 2cm of clear space between the insert and the glass on every side, and the flame should sit well below the spout and the top of the handle. If the flame would be level with the spout, add less sand or use a shorter insert.

Step 5: Light it with the lid off

Take the lid off and keep it off. A candle needs air, and the heat needs somewhere to go. With the lid on, the flame starves, smokes and blackens the glass, and the pot gets much hotter than it should.

Light the candle with a long lighter or a long match so your hand stays out of the pot. Then leave it where it is. Don’t pick the teapot up or move it while the candle is lit, because the glass, the handle and the sand all get hot.

Tip: If the flame flickers a lot or you see black soot on the glass, the opening is too small for the size of flame. Use a smaller insert, or a tealight instead of a poured tin.

Step 6: Lift the insert out to clean or refill

Blow the candle out and let everything cool completely. This takes longer than you’d think with glass, so give it at least half an hour. Then lift the insert straight out, refill it or drop in a new one, and wipe the inside of the glass with a soft cloth. A little washing-up liquid on the cloth deals with any smoky film. If you want to wash the teapot properly, tip the sand out into a bowl first and put it back once the pot is dry.

Safety

  • Only use heat-resistant glass. Ordinary decorative glass teapots are not made for heat.
  • Never pour wax straight into the teapot.
  • Always burn it with the lid off.
  • Keep the flame small and well below the spout and handle.
  • Don’t move the teapot while it’s lit or still hot.
  • Stand it on a heatproof mat, away from curtains, shelves and anything above it.
  • Never leave it burning in an empty room.

The no-flame version

Put an LED tealight in the insert instead and you can put the lid back on. This is the version to use on a bookshelf, in a child’s room or anywhere you’d worry about a real flame. A few LED tealights on a timer look lovely in a glass pot on a windowsill.

Using a china teapot

The same method works in a china teapot, using the lid opening as the top of a lantern. You won’t see the flame through the sides, so it looks more like a candle in a pot than a lantern, but it’s a good use for a pretty teapot that’s lost its cups. The same rules apply: insert, sand, lid off and a small flame. For smaller pieces of china, our teacup tealight holders guide is a quicker project.
