A teacup candle is a good Christmas present for people who are hard to buy for. It’s useful, it looks pretty on a mantelpiece or a dressing table, and once the wax has gone there’s still a lovely cup left over. This guide covers what to look for in a festive one, where to find them, and how to make your own to give. For the general things to check before you buy any teacup candle, see our main gift guide.

Order early

Most teacup candles are made by hand in small batches, and the cups are one-offs. Popular designs sell out in December and can’t simply be restocked. Small makers also tend to set their own last order dates for Christmas, often a week or two before Royal Mail’s last posting dates, so they have time to pour, cure and post everything. If you see one you like in November, buy it.

If you’re buying from eBay or Etsy, check where the seller is posting from. Something coming from abroad can take much longer at this time of year.

Festive scents

Christmas candle scents fall into a few families:

  • Spice: cinnamon, clove, nutmeg and gingerbread
  • Fruit: orange, clementine and cranberry, often mixed with spice
  • Forest: pine, fir and cedarwood
  • Church and incense: frankincense and myrrh

Spicy and fruity scents are the most popular, and a candle labelled something like “orange and cinnamon” is a safe bet for most people. Strong spice scents aren’t to everyone’s taste in a small room, though, so for someone you don’t know well, or someone with allergies or asthma, an unscented candle is kinder. A pretty cup with plain cream wax still looks like a proper present.

Festive colours and cups

Deep red, forest green and cream are the classic Christmas wax colours. They look best in cups with a gold rim or with red and green already in the pattern. Holly, robins and winter scenes turn up on vintage china too, often on cups made as Christmas sets. Plain white and gold cups are easy to make look festive with dark red or green wax.

Our colour palettes guide has a Christmas set of colours, and the black teacup candles guide covers black and gold, which suits a more dramatic table.

Where to buy them

Small UK makers are the best place for one-off cups and proper hand-poured candles. Several of the makers in our makers section sell teacup candles from their own shops or on Folksy, and some make Christmas ranges.

eBay is where you’ll find the most variety, from small makers and from people clearing out handmade stock. Search for Christmas teacup candles and check the photos carefully, because the cups are all different.

Amazon is easier if you need something quickly, particularly gift sets with a box. These are usually new china rather than vintage.

Whichever you choose, check what wax it is, what the scent is, whether a saucer is included and whether it comes in a gift box. A candle that’s been posted in bubble wrap still needs wrapping.

Making your own to give

A candle you’ve made yourself is about as personal as a present gets. You chose the cup with that person in mind, picked a colour and scent you knew they’d like, and spent an evening at the hob doing it. Our look at handmade candles versus the big-brand jar makes the case for handmade presents more generally.

A homemade teacup candle is a lovely present, and making a set of three or four for different people takes about the same time as making one. Our teacup candle instructable takes you through it step by step.

Give yourself time. The wax needs at least a day to set, and a scented candle smells much stronger if you let it cure for one to two weeks before it’s lit. So for Christmas, aim to pour by early December.

A few ideas that work well as presents:

  • A matching set. Three cups poured from one batch of wax at different strengths, from pale to deep. Our palettes guide explains how.
  • A cup that means something. A pattern from someone’s childhood, or a cup from a family tea set. Look out for odd cups in the same pattern at charity shops.
  • A decorated cup. A plain white cup painted with a name, a date or a few holly leaves. See decorating china yourself.
  • A teapot lantern. For a bigger present, a glass teapot lantern with a spare candle insert.

Wrapping and a care card

Teacup candles are awkward shapes to wrap. A small box lined with tissue paper or shredded paper keeps the cup safe and stops the handle snapping. Wrap the saucer separately and put it underneath.

It’s a nice touch to add a small card with how to look after the candle: trim the wick to 5mm, burn it for two to three hours the first time, never more than four hours at once, and stop when there’s 1cm of wax left. Our safety and care guide has the full list.

Mother’s Day

In the UK, Mothering Sunday falls three weeks before Easter, so in 2027 it’s on Sunday 7 March. Teacup candles make a good Mother’s Day present too, and the same advice about ordering early from small makers applies. For Mother’s Day, softer colours like blush, cream and lilac and lighter floral scents suit most people better than the deep Christmas colours. Our Mother’s Day teacup candles guide has more ideas.
