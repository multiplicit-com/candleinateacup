A candle making kit is the easiest way to try making candles, and a popular Christmas and birthday present. The trouble is that “candle making kit” covers everything from a children’s mould set to a box of professional-looking supplies, and many of them aren’t much use if what you want to make is a candle in a vintage teacup. This guide explains what to look for, which kinds of kit we’d pick and which we’d skip, and when you’re better off buying the parts separately.

How we chose

We haven’t tested every kit on sale, and we don’t pretend to. Kits change their contents and suppliers often, so naming one box as “the best” would be out of date within months. Instead, we’ve picked by what’s in the box, because that decides whether a kit can make a good teacup candle. We looked at the wax type, whether the wicks are pre-tabbed and sized for containers, whether a pouring jug and thermometer are included, and how clear the instructions are. The links below take you to kits and supplies that match each pick, so you can compare current options and reviews before you buy.

Our top pick: a soy container wax kit with a jug and thermometer

The kind of kit we’d buy for teacup candles has:

  • Container wax, usually soy or a soy blend. It’s made to stick to the sides of a cup and burn slowly, which is exactly what a teacup candle needs.
  • Pre-tabbed cotton wicks, ideally in more than one size, plus wick stickers.
  • A metal pouring jug, which you stand in a pan of water to melt the wax.
  • A thermometer, because pouring temperature makes a real difference to the finish.
  • Fragrance oil made for candles, if you want scent, and a few dye chips if you want colour.
  • Clear instructions that give temperatures and amounts, not just pictures.

Our budget pick: skip the kit

If you’re buying for yourself and don’t mind gathering a few things, buying the basics separately usually costs less than a kit and gives you far more wax:

  • A 1kg bag of container soy wax, which fills five or six teacups.
  • A pack of pre-tabbed cotton wicks in a couple of sizes.
  • A metal pouring jug, ideally one with a thermometer.
  • Kitchen scales and an old saucepan, which you probably already have.

Good alternatives

Beeswax kits. Beeswax burns beautifully and smells faintly of honey, and beeswax kits make good presents. Beeswax needs a slightly bigger wick than soy and is harder to pour neatly, so it’s better as a second kit than a first. Check whether the kit uses beeswax pellets for pouring, which work in a teacup, or rolled sheets, which don’t.

Wax melt kits. These come with moulds or clamshells instead of containers and no wicks. They’re a good way to use up spare wax, but they won’t make a teacup candle.

Kits to skip for teacups

  • Mould kits for pillar candles, stars, cones and novelty shapes. They use harder wax that doesn’t suit containers.
  • Gel wax kits. Gel needs its own wicks and containers, and it isn’t a good match for old china.
  • Rolled beeswax sheet kits. These make rolled taper and pillar candles, not poured ones.
  • Children’s casting kits. Fine for a craft afternoon with supervision, but they’re mould kits by another name.

Before you buy one as a present

  • Check what’s not included. Many kits say “everything you need” but leave out the thermometer, the jug or the containers.
  • Look at the wax amount. A kit with only a few hundred grams of wax will make two or three teacup candles at most.
  • Read the instructions in the listing photos if you can. Clear temperatures and amounts are a good sign.
  • Think about who it’s for. For someone with allergies or asthma, choose an unscented kit or leave the fragrance out.
  • Add a cup. A pretty vintage cup and saucer tucked in with the kit turns it into a teacup candle present.
