Teacup candle instructable: make a candle in a vintage cup, step by step

This is our teacup candle instructable. It’s the same method as our longer how-to guide, set out one step at a time with a diagram for each, so you can keep it open on your phone while you work. If it’s your first candle, read it through once before you start, so you have everything to hand when the wax is hot.

DifficultyEasy
Time1 hour + 24 hours to set
Makes1 teacup candle

Materials

  • A china teacup, ideally with its saucer. Cups 7cm to 9cm across are the easiest.
  • Container soy wax, roughly 150g to 200g for an average teacup
  • A pre-tabbed cotton wick, about 10cm long
  • A wick sticker
  • Candle fragrance oil (optional). Most soy waxes take 6% to 10%, so check your wax’s limit.
  • Candle dye (optional)

Tools

  • A metal pouring jug and a saucepan to stand it in
  • A thermometer
  • Kitchen scales
  • A centring bar, two lolly sticks or a clothes peg
  • A hairdryer
  • Wick trimmers or sharp scissors

Step 1: Check and clean the cup

Run a finger round the rim and inside the bowl, and hold the cup up to the light to look for hairline cracks. A crack can open up once the wax gets hot, so put any damaged cups aside for something else. Then wash the cup in hot soapy water and dry it completely, because grease or water stops the wax sticking. Our teacup preparation guide goes into more detail on checking and cleaning cups.

Tip: Fine crazing in old glaze is usually fine. Avoid cups where the glaze is flaking.

Step 2: Work out how much wax you need

Fill the cup with water to about 1cm below the rim, pour the water into a jug on the scales and note the weight in grams. Soy wax is lighter than water, so multiply by 0.86 to get the weight of wax you need. A cup that holds 200g of water needs about 172g of wax. Add 10g to 20g extra for a top-up later, then dry the cup again. Our how much wax calculator does the sum for you, including fragrance.

Tip: If you're adding fragrance, it counts towards the total. For 170g at 8%, use about 156g of wax and 14g of fragrance oil.

Step 3: Fix the wick in the centre

Peel a wick sticker and press it onto the bottom of the metal tab. Then push the tab firmly onto the centre of the cup with the end of a pencil. Thread the top of the wick through a centring bar, or clip it in a clothes peg resting across the rim, so it stands straight.

Tip: Teacups curve at the bottom, so the centre can be hard to judge from above. Look at it from the side as well.

Step 4: Melt the wax

Put the wax in a metal pouring jug and stand it in a saucepan with a few centimetres of gently simmering water. Keep the thermometer in the wax and stir now and then. Don’t melt wax straight on the hob or in the microwave, as it can overheat quickly.

Tip: Never leave melting wax unattended, and keep the water topped up so the pan doesn't boil dry.

Step 5: Add the fragrance

Your wax supplier will give a temperature for adding fragrance. For many soy waxes it’s around 80°C to 85°C. Take the jug off the heat when the wax reaches it, add the fragrance oil (and dye, if you’re using it), and stir slowly for a full two minutes so it mixes in properly.

Tip: Use oils made for candles. Many essential oils fade or burn off at these temperatures.

Step 6: Warm the cup

While the wax cools, warm the cup with a hairdryer for about a minute, moving it around so the whole cup heats evenly. A cold cup makes the wax set too quickly at the sides, which leaves rough patches and gaps.

Step 7: Pour

Let the wax cool to its pouring temperature. For soy this is usually between 55°C and 65°C, but check your wax’s instructions. Pour slowly into the cup until it’s about 1cm below the rim. Check the wick is still straight and central, and nudge the centring bar if it isn’t.

Tip: Keep the extra wax in the jug. You may need it for a top-up in the next step.

Step 8: Let it set and trim the wick

Leave the candle somewhere at room temperature and out of draughts for at least 24 hours. Don’t put it in the fridge to speed things up, because that causes cracks and sinkholes. If a dip forms around the wick, melt the leftover wax and pour a thin layer over the top. Once it’s fully set, take off the centring bar and trim the wick to about 5mm.

Step 9: Cure, then do the first burn

Unscented candles can be lit straight away. Scented soy candles smell much stronger if you leave them for one to two weeks first. On the first burn, stand the cup on its saucer and let it burn until the melted wax reaches the edge of the cup. In a teacup this usually takes two to three hours, and it stops the candle tunnelling down the middle on later burns.

Tip: Burn for no more than four hours at a time, and stop when there's about 1cm of wax left. Our safety and care guide has the rest.

If something goes wrong

  • A hole or dip in the middle: the wax shrank as it cooled. Top it up with a thin layer of melted wax.
  • White patches on the surface: this is called frosting and is normal for soy. It doesn’t affect the burn.
  • Wax pulling away from the side: warm the cup for longer before you pour next time.
  • Tunnelling or a tiny flame: the wick is probably too small for the cup. Our wax and wicks guide explains how to choose a size.
