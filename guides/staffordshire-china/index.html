Why Staffordshire china is so popular, and rightly so

By Steven · Updated 22 November 2026

We may earn a commission from links on this page. How affiliate links work

  • Most of the English china you find in charity shops was made in and around Stoke-on-Trent, known as the Potteries.
  • English bone china is white, translucent and strong for its thickness, which suits a candle very well.
  • The backstamp on the bottom tells you the maker, often the pattern and sometimes roughly when it was made.
  • So much was made that good cups are still easy and cheap to find.

Turn over almost any pretty teacup in a British charity shop and there’s a good chance the backstamp says Staffordshire, Stoke-on-Trent, Longton or Burslem. This site is run by someone from Stoke, so we’re biased, but there are good reasons why Staffordshire china is the first thing most teacup candle makers reach for.

The Potteries

Stoke-on-Trent is really six towns: Tunstall, Burslem, Hanley, Stoke, Fenton and Longton. They joined together as one borough in 1910 and became a city in 1925. Locally the whole area is still called the Potteries, and for a couple of hundred years it was the centre of the British pottery industry. Our guide to the Six Towns tells their story in more detail.

It grew up there for practical reasons. North Staffordshire had coal to fire the kilns and local clay for everyday earthenware. As the industry grew, finer clays were brought in from Cornwall and Devon, and the Trent and Mersey Canal, which Josiah Wedgwood campaigned for and which opened in 1777, made it much easier to bring materials in and get fragile pottery out to the ports.

At its height the skyline was full of bottle ovens, the brick kilns shaped like giant bottles. A few survive. You can walk into one at the Gladstone Pottery Museum in Longton, which is a complete Victorian pottery. The Potteries Museum and Art Gallery in Hanley has a huge collection of local ceramics, and Middleport Pottery in Burslem still makes Burleigh ware the traditional way.

Bone china

The English invention that matters most for teacups is bone china. It’s made with a large proportion of bone ash mixed with china clay and china stone, and fired to make something very white, slightly translucent and surprisingly strong for how thin it is. Josiah Spode’s firm in Stoke is generally credited with perfecting the recipe around 1800, and it became the standard for good English tea sets.

Those qualities are exactly what you want for a candle. The white body makes the colours of the pattern and the wax look clean, the thin walls let a little candlelight glow through, and the strength means a sound cup copes with the warmth of a candle without trouble.

You can usually tell bone china by holding it up to the light with your hand behind it. You’ll see the shadow of your fingers through the china. Earthenware is thicker and opaque.

The famous makers

Hundreds of firms worked in the Potteries over the years. These are some of the names you’ll see most often on teacups.

  • Royal Albert grew out of Thomas Wild’s pottery in Longton. Its Old Country Roses pattern, launched in 1962, is one of the best-known china patterns in the world, and it’s easy to find secondhand.
  • Wedgwood was founded by Josiah Wedgwood in Burslem in 1759. The firm later built its Etruria works, and moved to Barlaston in 1940, where the World of Wedgwood visitor centre is now.
  • Spode, from the town of Stoke, is famous for blue and white transfer-printed patterns such as Blue Italian, as well as for bone china.
  • Royal Doulton started in Lambeth in London and opened a factory in Burslem in the late 1870s, which became its main home for china.
  • Aynsley of Longton made fine bone china tea sets, often with fruit, flowers and generous gilding.
  • Paragon, also of Longton, made elegant floral bone china and held royal warrants.
  • Shelley is loved by collectors for its fine cups, including Art Deco shapes, and good pieces tend to cost more.

Plenty of smaller makers, such as Colclough, Duchess, Royal Stafford, Queen Anne and Royal Standard, made pretty bone china too, and their cups are often cheaper because the names are less famous. For a candle, a cup you like the look of matters more than the name on the bottom.

Reading a backstamp

The backstamp is the mark on the bottom of the cup or saucer. It usually gives the maker’s name and a logo, and often the pattern name or a pattern number. A few general clues help with dating, though they are only rough guides.

  • “England” on its own was added to many exports after 1891, when the United States began requiring the country of origin to be marked.
  • “Made in England” is generally a 20th-century mark.
  • “Bone China” or “Fine Bone China” on the stamp is common on 20th-century cups.
  • “Rd No” followed by a number is a registered design number, used from 1884.

Makers changed their backstamps over the years, so for most firms you can narrow down the date further by matching the stamp to a reference site or book. Cups and saucers in a “trio” with a plate don’t always match each other, so check the stamp on each piece.

Why they’re so easy to find

Because the Potteries made so much china for so long, an enormous amount of it is still around. A lot of it was wedding presents and best china that came out once a year, so it’s often in good condition. Single cups and saucers without the rest of the set turn up for very little at charity shops, car boot sales, house clearances and online auctions. That makes them ideal for candles. You can afford to pick only sound cups with patterns you love, and you aren’t breaking up a set that someone would want whole.

For tips on checking cups before you buy, see our teacup preparation guide, and for looking after them once you have them, caring for old china.

Visiting Stoke? As well as the museums, several factories have shops and run tours, including Emma Bridgewater in Hanley, Middleport Pottery and the World of Wedgwood. Check opening times before you go, as they change through the year.
