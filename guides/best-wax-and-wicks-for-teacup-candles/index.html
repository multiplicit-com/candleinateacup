Choosing wax and wicks for teacup candles

Wax and wick choice decide whether a teacup candle burns evenly or leaves a thick ring of wax stuck to the sides. The cup itself doesn’t change much about the method, but china holds heat differently from glass, and teacups are wider and shallower than most candle jars, so it’s worth getting the combination right.

Wax

Container soy wax. This is what most people use for teacup candles. It melts at a fairly low temperature, holds fragrance well, sticks to the sides of the cup and is easy to clean off with hot water. Make sure you buy a wax described as a container wax rather than a pillar wax. Pillar waxes are harder and designed to stand on their own, and they shrink away from the cup.

Rapeseed wax. A common choice for UK makers because the rapeseed is grown here. It behaves a lot like soy and is often sold as a blend with it.

Coconut and coconut blends. Creamy and smooth on top with a good scent throw, but usually more expensive. Often blended with soy or rapeseed.

Paraffin. Cheap and very good at throwing scent, and it gives a glossy finish. It does tend to soot more if the wick is a bit too big, and a lot of buyers now look for plant waxes.

Beeswax. Burns hot and slow with a natural honey smell. It’s harder to wick in a wide cup and is often mixed with another wax. Pure beeswax is expensive for a beginner’s project.

For a fuller comparison, including blends and gel wax, see our candle wax types guide.

Wicks

Most teacup candles use flat-braided cotton wicks with a metal tab at the bottom. Wick series have names like ECO, CD, HTP and LX, and each comes in a range of sizes. Each series burns slightly differently, but any of them can work. The main thing is picking the right size within a series.

If you want a black or coloured wick, see the section on coloured wicks. Wooden wicks are popular for their crackle, but they need a bit more trial and error in a round cup and can be fussy with soy.

Sizing a wick for a teacup

Measure the inside width of the cup at the level the wax will come up to. Then look at your wick supplier’s sizing chart for your wax type, or use our wick size calculator for a starting point. The charts are a starting point. Strong fragrance loads, dyes and the shape of the cup all change how a wick behaves.

For cups wider than about 9cm, one wick may not be able to melt all the way to the edge. Two smaller wicks set apart can work better. Our guide to multi-wick candles explains how to size and place them.

The test burn

The only reliable way to check a wick is to burn a test candle. Let it cure first if it’s scented, then light it and watch it for three to four hours.

  • Wick too small: the flame is small, and the melt pool hasn’t reached the edges after three or four hours. The candle will tunnel down the middle and waste the wax around the sides.
  • About right: the flame is steady and roughly 1cm to 2cm tall, and the melt pool reaches the edge of the cup within about three hours without getting very deep.
  • Wick too big: the flame is tall or flickers, the wick grows a black “mushroom” on top, there’s visible soot, or the cup gets very hot to touch.

Write down the cup size, wax, fragrance load and wick for each test. It’s tedious, but after a few rounds you’ll know what works for the cups you use most.
