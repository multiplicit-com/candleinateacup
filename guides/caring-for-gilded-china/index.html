Caring for gold and platinum rims on china, and whether you can restore them

A gold rim is often what makes an old teacup look special, especially by candlelight. It’s also the first thing to wear. This guide explains what gilding actually is, how to keep it looking good, and what you can do when it’s already worn. Some fixes are realistic at home and some aren’t, and we’ll be honest about which is which.

What gilding is

Gold and platinum decoration on china is real metal, applied over the glaze and then fired in a kiln at a lower temperature than the glaze itself. The firing fuses it to the surface. It’s very thin, which is why it wears.

Burnished gold is the traditional kind, found on better old china. It’s a thicker layer, with more gold in it, that comes out of the kiln dull and is then polished by hand to a soft, rich shine. It lasts well and has a warm, slightly matt glow.

Bright gold, also called liquid gold, is a thinner film that comes out of the kiln already shiny, so it needs no polishing. It was cheaper to apply, and it’s on a lot of 20th-century china. It looks bright and mirror-like when new but wears through more quickly.

Platinum and “silver” rims on china are usually platinum, not silver, because platinum doesn’t tarnish. They look silver-grey. Real silver decoration does exist but is less common, and it tarnishes like any silver.

Lustre is a thin metallic or pearly finish that covers a larger area, such as the copper-coloured lustreware made in Staffordshire and Sunderland. It’s just as delicate as gilding.

What damages it

  • The dishwasher. Strong detergents and heat wear gold and platinum away remarkably fast. Even a few cycles can dull it. This is the main reason so many rims are worn.
  • The microwave. Metal decoration can spark, which scorches the gold and can crack the cup.
  • Scourers, cream cleaners and bicarbonate of soda pastes. Anything abrasive polishes the gold off.
  • Bleach and strong cleaners. They can attack the gilding and the glaze beneath it.
  • Metal polish. It’s abrasive. Don’t use it on gold or platinum rims, even if they look dull.
  • Stacking. The foot of one cup rubs the gold off the rim of the cup below. Put paper or felt between pieces.

Cleaning gilded china

Wash it by hand in warm water with a little washing-up liquid, using a soft cloth or sponge. Don’t leave it to soak. Rinse, then dry it straight away with a soft tea towel, so water marks don’t build up on the metal. A very soft brush helps with dirt in raised gold patterns.

If a gold rim looks dull, it’s often just a film of grease or soot. Washing it properly usually brings it back. If it still looks dull or pinkish, the gold has probably worn thin, and cleaning harder will only make it worse.

Candles and gold rims

A well-made teacup candle shouldn’t harm a gold rim. Fired gold copes with far more heat than a candle gives off, and if the wax is about 1cm below the rim and the wick is the right size, the flame stays well inside the cup.

What does mark a rim is soot. A wick that’s too long or too big smokes and leaves a black film on the china. Trim the wick to about 5mm before each lighting, and wipe the rim gently with a soft damp cloth and a little washing-up liquid once it’s cool. Don’t scrub. Our guide to wax and wicks explains how to tell if your wick is too big.

Don’t dig old wax out of a gilded cup with a knife. Freeze it or float it out with warm water instead. See refilling a teacup candle.

Can you restore worn gold?

Sometimes, depending on how much time and money you want to spend. Here are the options, from the most lasting to the least.

Liquid gold, fired in a kiln

This is how gilding is done properly. Liquid gold for china painting contains real gold in an oily medium. You paint it on with a fine brush and then fire the piece in a kiln, usually at somewhere around 700°C to 800°C depending on the product, which burns off the medium and fuses the gold to the glaze. The result is real, durable gold.

The honest catches:

  • You need a kiln. Many pottery studios and some china painting groups will fire pieces for you for a small fee.
  • It’s expensive. A small bottle costs a lot, because it’s real gold.
  • The fumes. Liquid gold contains solvents. Work with a window open and read the safety data sheet.
  • It takes practice. A rim needs a steady hand, and mistakes show once fired.
  • Lips and food. Check the product’s instructions about rims and food use. For a cup that will only ever be a candle, this matters less.
  • Old cups and kilns. A cup that’s cracked or crazed may not survive another firing.

Oven-baked gold pens and paints

Porcelain paint pens and paints in gold are fixed by baking in a home oven, usually at around 150°C to 160°C. They’re easy and cheap. But they aren’t real gold, they look more like gold paint than fired gilding, and they aren’t as hard-wearing. Use them for decoration on the outside of the cup or the saucer. Our guide to decorating china yourself covers these in detail.

Gold leaf and cold gilding

Gold leaf or imitation gold leaf stuck on with gilding size, and gold acrylic paints, need no heat at all. They’re purely decorative. They’ll rub off with washing and handling, and they shouldn’t go on the rim or anywhere near the flame, where the size or paint could soften or scorch. They’re fine for a display piece or the outside of a saucer.

A professional restorer

For a valuable or sentimental piece, a ceramics restorer can regild it properly. It costs more than an ordinary vintage cup is worth, so it’s usually only worth it for something special. In the UK, the Institute of Conservation runs the Conservation Register, which lists accredited conservators.

Being realistic

For an everyday cup that’s going to become a candle, the simplest answer is usually to leave the gold as it is. A little wear on the rim is a sign of a cup that was used and loved for decades, and in candlelight it hardly shows. If the gold matters to you, choose cups with good gilding to start with. Hold them up to the light at the charity shop and look for an even, unbroken rim.
