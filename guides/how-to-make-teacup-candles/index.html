How to make a teacup candle

By Steven · Updated 22 November 2026

We may earn a commission from links on this page. How affiliate links work

  • Use container soy wax, pre-tabbed cotton wicks and a fragrance oil made for candles.
  • Weigh the water a cup holds and multiply by about 0.86 to get the weight of wax you need.
  • Warm the cup before pouring and let the candle set slowly at room temperature.
  • Wait a week or two before burning a scented soy candle so the scent has time to develop.

Making a candle in a teacup is one of the easier candle projects to start with. The cup does the hard work of looking nice, and a wide, shallow cup is a forgiving shape for a beginner. You can do the whole thing in a kitchen in about an hour, plus a day for the wax to set.

Choosing a cup

Almost any china teacup will work, but check it carefully first. Run a finger round the rim and inside the bowl, and hold it up to the light to look for hairline cracks. A crack that doesn’t matter on a shelf can open up once the wax gets hot. Crazing (the fine web of lines in old glaze) is usually fine, but avoid cups where it’s heavy or the glaze is flaking.

Wider cups are easier to get right than tall, narrow ones, because the melt pool can reach the sides more easily. Cups between about 7cm and 9cm across the top are a good size to start with. Odd cups without saucers are often cheap at charity shops and car boot sales, and you’ll find plenty on eBay too.

How much wax you need

The easiest way to work this out is to fill the cup with water to where you want the wax to stop, usually about 1cm below the rim, then pour the water into a jug on a kitchen scale. Soy wax is a bit lighter than water, so multiply the weight of the water by about 0.86. A cup that holds 200g of water will need roughly 170g of wax. Add 10 to 20 grams extra so you don’t run short.

If you’re adding fragrance, the fragrance oil counts as part of the total. Most container soy waxes take between 6% and 10% fragrance, but check the maximum your wax supplier gives, because some waxes sweat or won’t hold the oil above a certain amount.

Picking a wick

Wick size depends on the width of the cup, the wax and the fragrance, so there isn’t one right answer. Most wick suppliers publish a chart that suggests a size for a given diameter, and that’s the best place to start. Measure across the inside of the cup at the top of where the wax will be.

If you’re new to it, buy a mixed pack of pre-tabbed cotton wicks and make a test candle before you make a batch. Our guide to wax and wicks explains how to tell whether a wick is too big or too small.

Step by step

  1. Clean the cup. Wash it in hot soapy water and dry it completely. Any grease or water can stop the wax sticking.
  2. Fix the wick. Put a wick sticker on the metal tab and press it firmly into the centre of the cup. Use a centring bar, two lolly sticks or a clothes peg resting across the rim to keep the wick straight.
  3. Melt the wax. Put the wax in a metal pouring jug and stand it in a pan of gently simmering water. Don’t melt wax directly on the hob, as it can overheat quickly. Keep a thermometer in it.
  4. Add the fragrance. Your wax supplier will give a temperature for adding fragrance, often somewhere around 80°C to 85°C for soy. Take the jug off the heat, add the oil and stir slowly for a couple of minutes so it binds with the wax.
  5. Warm the cup. While the wax cools, warm the cup with a hairdryer for a minute or so. A cold cup makes the wax set too quickly against the sides, which can leave gaps and rough patches.
  6. Pour. When the wax has cooled to the pouring temperature (again, check your wax’s instructions, though 55°C to 65°C is typical for soy), pour slowly into the cup. Check the wick is still upright and central.
  7. Leave it alone. Let the candle set at room temperature, away from draughts, for at least 24 hours. Putting it in the fridge to speed things up tends to cause cracks and sinkholes.
  8. Trim and cure. Trim the wick to about 5mm. Unscented candles can be burned straight away, but scented soy candles smell much stronger if you leave them for one to two weeks first.

Common problems

A dip or hole in the middle. This is usually because the wax shrank as it cooled. Keep back a little wax, and once the candle has set, melt it again and pour a thin top-up layer. A hairdryer passed over the surface can also smooth small dips.

Frosting or white patches. Soy wax often develops a white bloom. It’s harmless and doesn’t affect how the candle burns. Pouring a little cooler and letting the candle cool slowly helps.

Wax pulling away from the side. You’ll see this as patches that look wet through the china, if the cup is thin enough to see through. Warming the cup before pouring usually stops it.

The candle tunnels when burned. The wick is too small for the cup, or the candle wasn’t burned long enough the first time. See the safety and care guide for how long to burn it.

If you want to sell them

Selling candles in the UK comes with labelling rules. Scented candles need hazard labelling under the CLP regulations, and every candle should carry basic safety warnings. If you’re thinking of selling at craft fairs or online, read up on the requirements first. Your fragrance supplier should be able to provide the safety data you need.
