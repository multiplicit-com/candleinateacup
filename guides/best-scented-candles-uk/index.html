Most people buy a scented candle by taking the lid off and sniffing it. That’s a fair start, but it doesn’t tell you much about how the candle will smell once it’s lit, in your living room, on a Tuesday evening. This guide explains what makes a candle fill a room with scent, the kinds we’d buy, and how to get the most from whichever one you choose.

The big brands. Big-brand jar candles are popular for good reason. They’re easy to find and very consistent. Whether they’re better than a handmade candle is mostly a matter of taste, and our comparison of handmade and big-brand candles tries to be fair to both.

Cold throw and hot throw

Cold throw is how a candle smells before it’s lit. Hot throw is how it smells when it’s burning and the top has melted. They’re not always the same. Some scents smell strong in the jar but fade in the room, and some only really open up once the wax is warm.

Hot throw depends mostly on the melted pool of wax, because that’s where the scent comes from. A candle with a wide, full melt pool gives off much more scent than one burning in a narrow hole down the middle. That’s why a well-sized wick matters as much as the fragrance itself. Reviews that mention how a candle smells once lit, and in what size of room, are much more useful than ones about the smell in the jar.

Which waxes carry scent best

  • Paraffin is very good at throwing scent, which is one reason it’s still widely used.
  • Coconut blends carry scent well and give a smooth, creamy top.
  • Soy and rapeseed are a little gentler but still fill a room well with a good fragrance and the right wick. Soy candles usually smell stronger after a week or two of curing.
  • Beeswax has its own honey smell and is usually sold unscented or with simple scents.

Our guide to candle wax types goes into more detail. None of these is right or wrong. A good candle in any of them will fill a room.

Matching the candle to the room

A small candle won’t fill a big open-plan room, however good the scent. As a rough guide, a tin or a teacup suits a bedroom, bathroom or study, and a bigger room needs a larger candle, a candle with more than one wick, or two candles at opposite ends. Open-plan rooms with high ceilings are the hardest of all.

What the label should tell you

Scented candles sold in Great Britain usually need hazard labelling under the CLP rules, with the seller’s details and a list of any allergens in the fragrance. A proper label is a good sign that the maker has done their homework. If you’re sensitive to particular scents, the allergen list is worth reading. Our guide to scenting teacup candles explains more about fragrance oils and essential oils.

Getting the most scent from any candle

  • Burn it long enough. Give it two to three hours so the melted wax reaches the edge. Short burns give little scent and can make the candle tunnel.
  • Shut the door. A closed room fills much faster than a draughty one.
  • Keep it out of draughts. A draught makes the flame flicker and smoke, and blows the scent away.
  • Trim the wick to about 5mm before each burn. A long wick smokes, and smoke covers the scent.
  • Don’t burn it for more than four hours at a time. It doesn’t give more scent, and the container gets hotter than it should.
