Caring for old china: cleaning, crazing, gold rims and getting wax out

By Steven · Updated 22 November 2026

Old teacups have usually survived decades of use already, and a bit of care keeps them going for decades more. This guide covers looking after vintage china in general, and getting a cup clean again once its candle has burned down so you can refill it or use it for something else.

Washing by hand

Warm water, a little washing-up liquid and a soft cloth or brush is all most china needs. Wash one or two pieces at a time in a bowl lined with a tea towel, which stops cups knocking against each other or the sink. Rinse in warm water and dry with a soft cloth, or leave them to drain.

Use warm water rather than very hot, and don’t take a cup straight from cold to hot. Sudden changes in temperature are what causes most new crazing in old glaze and can open up a crack you didn’t know was there. For the same reason, let a cup that’s been in a cold car or a cold outbuilding come up to room temperature before you wash it.

Why not the dishwasher

Dishwashers use strong detergents and high temperatures, and old decoration wasn’t made for either. Gold and platinum rims wear away and go dull, lustre glazes lose their shine, and colours applied over the glaze can fade. Even cups marked as dishwasher safe when they were new may not have the same glaze as modern china. If a cup matters to you, wash it by hand.

Keep cups with gold, silver or lustre out of the microwave as well. Metallic decoration can spark.

Looking after gold rims

Gold on old china is a very thin layer fired onto the glaze, and it wears off with rubbing. Wash gilded pieces gently, don’t use scourers or cream cleaners on them and don’t soak them for long periods. A soft cloth is enough. Dry them straight away so water marks don’t build up on the gold.

Lifting tea stains

Brown tea stains on the inside of old cups usually come out with a soak. Fill the cup with warm water and a spoonful of bicarbonate of soda, leave it for an hour or so and then wipe it with a soft cloth. Denture cleaning tablets dissolved in warm water are another gentle option that many collectors use.

Stains that have soaked into crazed glaze are harder to shift. There’s more on those below.

Crazing, and why it isn’t always a bad thing

Crazing is the fine web of lines you see in the glaze of a lot of old china. It happens because the glaze and the clay body under it expand and shrink by slightly different amounts as they warm and cool. Over decades of hot tea, cold cupboards and washing up, the glaze gives way in a network of hairline cracks. The cup itself is usually still sound.

For a lot of people, crazing is part of the appeal. It’s a sign that a cup is genuinely old and has been used, and it gives a soft, antique look that new china doesn’t have. Potters even make crackle glazes on purpose to get the same effect. On a candle cup it matters even less, because nobody is drinking from it and the wax covers most of the inside. A gently crazed cup with a pale wax in it can look lovelier than a pristine one.

Where crazing becomes a problem is when it’s stained dark brown, when the glaze is starting to flake or lift, or when the crazing is hiding a real crack in the body. Our teacup preparation guide explains how to check for that.

Can stained crazing be rescued?

The crazing itself can’t be undone. Once the glaze has crazed, the lines are there for good. What you can sometimes do is lighten the brown staining that has soaked into them, so the lines show less.

The method collectors most often use is a slow soak in hydrogen peroxide. The ordinary 3% solution sold in chemists is the safest to try.

  1. Put on rubber gloves and work somewhere airy.
  2. Stand the cup in a plastic or glass container and pour in enough hydrogen peroxide to cover the stained area.
  3. Cover the container to keep light out, as peroxide breaks down in light, and leave it at room temperature.
  4. Check it every day or so. It can take several days to a week or more for stains to fade, and some never go completely.
  5. Rinse the cup very well in plenty of clean water and let it dry thoroughly, for several days, before you use it for a candle.

You’ll find older advice online about using stronger peroxide from hairdressing suppliers and baking the soaked cup in the oven. We wouldn’t. Strong peroxide can cause chemical burns, and heating a wet, soaked cup can stress the glaze or crack an old piece. Some pottery specialists who used to recommend that method no longer do.

A few more warnings:

  • Never use chlorine bleach on crazed china. It may lighten the stain, but it gets into the body under the glaze, and over time it can make the glaze flake away.
  • Don’t soak cups with gold, lustre or hand-painted decoration over the glaze, or anything that’s been repaired. The soak can lift the decoration and soften old glue.
  • Try it on a cheap cup first, so you know what to expect.
  • Stop if the glaze starts to lift or flake, and rinse the cup straight away.

If a piece is valuable or has sentimental value, it’s worth asking a ceramics conservator before you try anything. They can tell you what’s safe for that particular piece.

Getting old wax out of a teacup candle

When a teacup candle has burned down to its last centimetre or so, it’s time to stop and clean it out. There are two good ways.

The freezer. Put the cup in the freezer for a few hours. The wax shrinks as it gets cold and usually comes away from the china in one piece with a gentle push from a butter knife or the handle of a teaspoon. Let the cup come back to room temperature slowly before you wash it.

Hot water. Fill the cup with hot water from the tap, not boiling water from the kettle, which is too much of a shock for old china. The wax softens and floats to the top. Leave it to cool, then lift off the disc of wax. Pour the water away outside or onto the garden rather than down the sink, as any bits of wax can block the drain.

Either way, pick out the old wick tab, wipe out any film of wax with kitchen paper, then wash the cup in warm soapy water. Your leftover wax can go into a new candle. See our guide to melting old candles.

Storing cups

Store cups the right way up. Rims are the most fragile part, and standing a cup upside down puts its weight on the rim. Don’t stack cups inside each other unless you put a sheet of kitchen paper or a paper napkin between them, and keep stacks short. Hanging cups by their handles on hooks looks nice on a dresser, but handles on old cups can be weaker than they look, particularly where they’ve been glued, so it’s safer for cups you don’t mind losing.

Saucers and plates can be stacked with paper or felt between them.

When to stop using a cup

Retire a cup from candle making, or from drinking, if it has a crack, a chip with a crack running from it, or glaze that’s flaking. It can still hold dried flowers, pens or an LED tealight.
