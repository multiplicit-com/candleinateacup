Teacup candles are everywhere now, from small makers with a few dozen cups to big online shops selling them by the thousand. Some are beautifully made and burn evenly to the last centimetre. Others look lovely in the photo and tunnel straight down the middle. This guide is about telling them apart, with the kinds we’d buy and what to look for in a listing.

Good alternatives

Unscented teacup candles. For a dining table, a café or anyone who doesn’t get on with strong scents, an unscented candle gives the look and the light without anything competing with the food. Many small makers will leave the scent out if you ask them before ordering.

Wide cups with two wicks. Breakfast cups and wide bowls are often too wide for one wick to melt all the way across. A good maker uses two smaller wicks set apart. Our guide to multi-wick candles explains why.

Black and gothic cups. For something less flowery, see our guide to black teacup candles.

Fine gilded china. For a special occasion, our guide to luxury teacup candles covers what’s worth paying more for.

More makers. Our makers section lists independent teacup candle makers in the UK and further afield.

How to judge a listing

You can tell a lot before you buy.

The wax. Container soy, rapeseed, coconut blends and paraffin all work in a cup. What matters is that the seller tells you which one it is. Our guide to candle wax types explains the differences.

The wick. In the photos, the wick should be in the centre of the cup and standing straight. A wick pushed over to one side burns unevenly and can overheat the china on that side.

The fill. The wax should stop a little below the rim, roughly a centimetre, with a smooth, level top. A small amount of white frosting on soy is normal and doesn’t affect the burn.

The cup. Look closely at photos of the inside. Crazing in the glaze is common in old china and usually fine, but a crack you can see from both sides isn’t, as it can open up with heat. Our guide to preparing teacups explains which faults matter.

The label. Scented candles sold in Great Britain usually need hazard labelling under the CLP rules, with the maker’s details and a list of allergens. Our guide to trade suppliers explains more. A candle sold with no safety information at all is a warning sign.

Care instructions. A good seller tells you how long to burn it, how short to keep the wick and when to stop. If the listing says nothing about it, check our safety and care guide.

Once it arrives

The first burn tells you whether the candle was well made. Burn it for two to three hours and look at the melted wax. If it reaches the edge of the cup or very close, the wick is the right size. If it stays as a small pool in the middle after three hours, the wick is too small and the candle will tunnel. A tall, flickering flame or black smoke usually means the wick needs trimming to 5mm, or is too big for the cup. Our guide to choosing wax and wicks has diagrams of what each looks like.

When the candle is finished, the cup can be cleaned and filled again. Our guide to refilling a teacup candle shows how.
