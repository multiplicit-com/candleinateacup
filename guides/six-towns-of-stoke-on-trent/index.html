The Six Towns of Stoke-on-Trent: how the Potteries made the nation's teacups

By Steven · Updated 22 November 2026

We may earn a commission from links on this page. How affiliate links work

  • Stoke-on-Trent is six towns that joined together in 1910. Arnold Bennett's novels call them the Five Towns, because he left out Fenton.
  • The pottery industry grew there because of local clay and coal, and the Trent and Mersey Canal, opened in 1777, got the china out safely.
  • Josiah Wedgwood's Etruria works was one of the earliest modern factories, and bone china was perfected in the town of Stoke.
  • Some 47 bottle ovens survive, and you can still visit working potteries, museums and factory shops.

If you’ve ever turned over a teacup and read “Made in England” on the bottom, there’s a good chance it was made within a few miles of the middle of Stoke-on-Trent. For around two hundred years, a string of small towns in North Staffordshire made much of the world’s pottery, from everyday plates to the finest bone china. This site is run by someone from Stoke, so forgive a little local pride, but it’s a story worth knowing if you love old cups.

Our guide to Staffordshire china covers the makers and how to read a backstamp. This one is about the place.

Six towns, or five?

Stoke-on-Trent is made up of six towns: Tunstall, Burslem, Hanley, Stoke, Fenton and Longton. Each grew up around its own potbanks, with its own town hall, market and football rivalries. They were joined together as a single county borough on 31 March 1910, and Stoke-on-Trent became a city in 1925. Locally, the whole area is still called the Potteries.

So why do so many people talk about the Five Towns? That’s down to Arnold Bennett, the novelist, who was born in Hanley in 1867. His books, starting with Anna of the Five Towns in 1902, are set in thinly disguised versions of the Potteries towns, and he left Fenton out. He explained that “Five Towns” simply sounded better than “Six Towns”, and that Fenton was the least important of them at the time. Fenton has never quite forgiven him.

Why here?

The industry grew up in North Staffordshire for good practical reasons.

  • Clay. The area had its own clays, good for the coarse earthenware and the red and buff pots that local potters had made for centuries.
  • Coal. Firing pottery takes a huge amount of fuel, and the North Staffordshire coalfield was right underneath the towns.
  • Water and transport. This was the weak point. Before the canal, fine clay from Cornwall and Devon had to come by sea and river and then by packhorse, and finished pottery went out the same way, which broke a lot of it.

The Trent and Mersey Canal solved that. Josiah Wedgwood was one of its main promoters, and he cut the first sod at Brownhills, Burslem, on 26 July 1766. Engineered by James Brindley, it was completed in 1777 and linked the Potteries to the ports of Liverpool and Hull. China clay could come in by boat, and plates and cups could go out by water, far more cheaply and with far fewer breakages.

Josiah Wedgwood and the factory

Josiah Wedgwood was born in Burslem in 1730, into a family of potters. He set up on his own in 1759 and became the most famous potter in the country.

His cream-coloured earthenware won an order for a tea set from Queen Charlotte, and from 1766 he sold it as Queen’s Ware. In the 1770s he developed jasperware, the matt stoneware best known in pale blue with white classical figures, which is still made today.

In 1769 he opened a new works beside the canal, between Hanley and Stoke, and named it Etruria. It’s remembered as one of the earliest modern factories. Rather than each potter making a piece from start to finish, the work was divided into stages, with each worker skilled in one job. The buildings were laid out in the order the work flowed, and the canal ran right past the door. Along with his business partner, Thomas Bentley, he also pioneered showrooms, catalogues and the idea that a famous customer could sell a pattern.

That’s why the Potteries matter in the story of the Industrial Revolution. Alongside the cotton mills and ironworks, Stoke showed how a traditional craft could be organised into an industry, and how canals could make it pay. The Wedgwood company moved to a new factory at Barlaston, just outside the city, in 1940, where the World of Wedgwood is today.

Bottle ovens

For two centuries the skyline was dominated by bottle ovens, the brick kilns shaped like giant bottles. The bottle was the outer chimney. Inside was the oven, stacked with pottery in fireclay boxes called saggars, and fired with coal for days at a time. When they were all burning, the smoke turned the towns black.

There were around two thousand of them at their peak, in the first half of the 20th century. After the Clean Air Act of 1956, the industry switched to gas and electric kilns, and the last coal firings took place in the 1960s. Most of the ovens were knocked down. Some 47 survive, and all of them are now listed buildings.

Bone china and the “pretty” years

The cups most of us reach for when making candles are bone china, and that was perfected in the Potteries too. Porcelain made with bone ash was first made at the Bow factory in London in the 1740s, but it was in the town of Stoke that Josiah Spode developed the recipe that became the standard, around 1790. His son, Josiah Spode II, made it a commercial success, and the name “bone china” stuck. Minton, also in Stoke, began making it around 1798.

Bone china is white, slightly translucent and strong for its thickness, which made it perfect for delicate tea sets. Through the 19th and 20th centuries, the Potteries made it in enormous quantities, covered in roses, violets, chintz patterns, gold rims and all the pretty decoration we now find in charity shops. A bone china tea set was a classic wedding present for generations of British families, which is why so many survive in good condition. Our Staffordshire china guide explains how to recognise it.

The makers, town by town

Hundreds of firms worked in the six towns. Here are some of the names you’ll find on cups, and where they were based.

  • Tunstall: Alfred Meakin and W. H. Grindley, both known for earthenware tableware, much of it exported.
  • Burslem: the “mother town” of the Potteries, where Wedgwood was born. Royal Doulton’s china works were in Nile Street, Moorcroft still makes its art pottery here, and Burleigh ware is made at Middleport Pottery.
  • Hanley: the biggest town and today’s city centre. Johnson Brothers were based here, and Wedgwood’s Etruria works stood on its edge. Emma Bridgewater makes its pottery here today.
  • Stoke: the town that gave the city its name, and home to Spode and Minton.
  • Fenton: the town Bennett forgot, but not a small one for pottery. Thomas Whieldon, who was Wedgwood’s partner for a few years, worked here, and so did the Mason family, who patented Mason’s Ironstone China in 1813.
  • Longton: the great bone china town, packed with small works and bottle ovens. Aynsley, Royal Albert, Paragon, Colclough, Duchess and many more were made here. Shelley’s Foley works stood in the area called the Foley, on the border between Fenton and Longton.

For a candle, Longton bone china is hard to beat. There’s a lot of it, it’s pretty, and a single cup without its saucer can cost very little.

What you can see today

Much of the industry has gone, but a good deal survives, and it’s well worth a visit.

  • Gladstone Pottery Museum, Longton. A complete Victorian potbank with bottle ovens you can walk into, and displays on how pottery was made and fired.
  • The Potteries Museum and Art Gallery, Hanley. A huge collection of Staffordshire ceramics, along with part of the Staffordshire Hoard of Anglo-Saxon gold. Check opening times before you go.
  • Middleport Pottery, Burslem. A working Victorian pottery by the canal where Burleigh is still made, with tours and a shop. You might recognise it from The Great Pottery Throw Down.
  • Moorcroft, Burslem. A heritage visitor centre and factory shop for its hand-decorated art pottery.
  • Emma Bridgewater, Hanley. Factory tours, a shop and a decorating studio where you can paint your own piece.
  • World of Wedgwood, Barlaston. A museum, factory tours and shops, just outside the city.
  • The British Ceramics Biennial. A festival of contemporary ceramics held every two years since 2009, mainly at the old Spode works in Stoke.

Several factories have shops selling seconds, which is a good place to find cups for candles. Opening times change through the year, so check before you travel.

Finding Stoke-made cups for candles

Turn the cup over. The backstamp will usually name the maker, and often the town: Longton, Burslem, Fenton and Stoke-on-Trent are all common. “Made in England” and “Bone China” or “Fine Bone China” tell you it’s probably 20th century. Our Staffordshire china guide explains what the different marks mean.

Charity shops, car boot sales and house clearances are full of them. Online, single cups and trios from Royal Albert, Aynsley, Paragon, Shelley and dozens of smaller Longton makers come up all the time, and the live listings above show a few.
