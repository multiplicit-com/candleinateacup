Fragrance is where a lot of candle makers spend the most money and have the most fun. It’s also where the cheap end of the market is at its most patchy. A good fragrance oil fills a room. A poor one barely smells once the candle is lit, or isn’t made to be heated in wax at all. This guide explains what to look for and the kinds of oil we’d buy.

How we chose

There are thousands of fragrance oils on sale, and scent is personal, so we haven’t tried to pick individual scents. We’ve picked by type and by supplier, looking at what makes an oil safe and reliable in a candle: whether it’s made for wax, whether the seller tells you how much to use and whether it comes with the safety information you need. What to check.

  • That it’s sold for candles, or for candles and wax melts.
  • The maximum usage rate, sometimes given as a percentage for candles.
  • That a safety data sheet is available. Many suppliers also give an IFRA certificate, which shows how much is safe to use in different kinds of product.
  • Whether it discolours wax. Vanilla and some bakery scents turn white wax cream or brown over time.

Oils to avoid

  • Oil burner and diffuser oils. These are often diluted in a carrier or in water and aren’t made to be heated in wax. They can weaken the scent, stop the wax setting properly or make the candle smoke.
  • Bath and body oils. Made for skin, not for burning.
  • Anything with no safety information at all. If a seller can’t tell you how much to use or provide a safety data sheet, choose another seller.

Essential oils

Essential oils can be used in candles, but they’re often weaker in wax than fragrance oils, because many of them evaporate easily and fade in the heat. They also need hazard labelling just like fragrance oils, often more of it. If you’d like a natural scent, look for essential oils or blends sold specifically for candle making by a candle supplier. Our guide to scenting teacup candles explains the difference in more detail, and our guide to eucalyptus and menthol candles covers fresh scents.

Scents “inspired by” famous perfumes

Many suppliers sell oils described as inspired by a well-known perfume or a big-brand candle. They’re popular and some are very good. The brand names belong to their owners, though, so if you sell candles, describe the scent in your own words rather than using the brand’s name.

How much to use

Fragrance is measured by weight as a percentage of the whole candle. Most container waxes take about 6% to 10%, and you should never go over your wax supplier’s maximum. More oil doesn’t always mean more scent, and too much can seep out of the wax or make the candle smoke. Weigh it on digital scales. Our how much wax calculator works out the grams of wax and fragrance for any cup.

Add fragrance at the temperature your wax supplier gives, often around 80°C to 85°C for soy, and stir gently for a couple of minutes. Then let the candle cure for a week or two before you judge the scent.

Storing fragrance oils

Keep the bottles tightly closed, somewhere cool and dark, and out of reach of children and pets. Most oils keep well for a year or more. If one starts to smell different from when you bought it, try it in a test candle before using it in a batch.

If you sell

Scented candles sold in Great Britain usually need hazard labelling under the CLP rules, worked out from the fragrance supplier’s safety data sheet and the amount you use. Our guide to trade suppliers explains more, and it’s worth checking the current rules with your local Trading Standards.
