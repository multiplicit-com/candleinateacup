To saucer or not to saucer? Teacup candles with and without one

Most teacup candles you see for sale come on a saucer, and most of the cups you find in charity shops come without one. So sooner or later anyone making these has to decide whether the saucer matters. A saucer helps, but it isn’t essential, and there are good ways round not having one.

The case for a saucer

It keeps heat off the furniture. China gets warm right through as a candle burns, and towards the end of a burn the base of the cup can be hot enough to mark a polished table. The saucer puts a layer of china and a small air gap between the cup and the surface.

It catches drips. Container candles shouldn’t drip, but wax does sometimes creep over the rim if the wick is too big or the candle has been knocked. It’s much easier to scrape a bit of wax off a saucer than out of the grain of a wooden table.

It’s harder to knock over. A teacup has a small foot and a handle sticking out to one side, so it isn’t the most stable thing in the world. The saucer gives it a much wider base, and the well in the middle stops the cup sliding about.

It looks finished. A cup on its saucer looks like a proper present, and it’s what most people picture when they think of a teacup candle. If you’re selling, it usually lets you charge a bit more. Our gift guide suggests a saucer is worth paying a little extra for.

You can go further with a trio. A trio is a cup, saucer and side plate in the same pattern, which is how a lot of English tea sets were sold. The side plate can sit underneath as a little stand, hold a box of matches and a wick trimmer, or be sold alongside as a matching set. Trios often turn up at car boot sales and house clearances when the teapot and the rest of the set have long gone.

The case against

Cost. A matching cup and saucer usually costs more than a single cup. If you’re making a batch to sell, saucers can add up.

Finding a match. This is the big one. Odd cups and odd saucers are both easy to find, but finding a saucer that matches the cup you already have can take ages. Even within the same pattern, saucers changed shape over the years, so the cup may not sit neatly in the well.

Space. A saucer roughly doubles the footprint. On a narrow windowsill or a crowded bathroom shelf, a cup on its own may simply fit better.

Posting. A saucer is one more piece of china to wrap and one more thing to break in the post.

Mismatched pairs

You don’t have to match. Plenty of people pair cups and saucers on purpose, and a mismatched set can look more interesting than a matching one. A few things help it look chosen rather than random:

  • Pick one thing in common. The same gold rim, the same background colour or the same kind of flower ties two different patterns together.
  • Plain with patterned. A busy cup on a plain white or cream saucer always works, and so does the reverse.
  • Similar age and weight. A fine 1950s bone china cup looks odd on a thick modern stoneware saucer. Keep fine with fine.
  • Check the fit. The foot of the cup should sit in the well without rocking. Take the cup with you when you go looking, or at least its measurements.

If you’re selling mismatched pairs, say so in the listing. Some buyers love them, but nobody wants to feel they were sold a matching set that wasn’t.

Should you glue the cup to the saucer?

Some makers glue the cup down so the whole thing is one piece. Others won’t, and on balance we wouldn’t either.

What gluing gets you:

  • The cup can’t slide off or rattle about when it’s carried or posted.
  • It travels and displays as one piece, which suits craft events where people pick things up.
  • It can’t be knocked off the saucer.

Why many makers avoid it:

  • The cup and saucer can’t be washed or used separately afterwards. A lot of people like to clean out the cup and reuse it, or put the china back in the cupboard, and our guide on caring for old china assumes you can.
  • Ordinary glue softens or lets go when it gets warm, and the base of the cup does get warm.
  • Glue round the foot can show, and it’s hard to get off old china cleanly if you change your mind.
  • Collectors and buyers who care about vintage china won’t thank you for glued pieces.

If you do glue it:

  • Use an adhesive made for ceramics, and check the packet for a temperature rating. You want one that says it copes with heat, well above the warmth of a cup of tea.
  • Don’t use hot glue. It softens again as soon as it warms up, which is exactly when you need it to hold.
  • Superglue isn’t a good choice either. It’s brittle on china and doesn’t like heat.
  • Use a small amount, only on the foot ring, and let it cure fully before you pour any wax. The packet will say how long.
  • Tell the buyer it’s glued.

A gentler option: a few drops of the same candle wax on the saucer, with the cup pressed into them while they’re still soft, will hold the cup in place for carrying and posting. It lifts off cleanly later with a twist or a little warm water.

No saucer? What to stand it on

A cup with no saucer needs something heatproof under it every time it’s lit. Any of these will do:

  • A side plate or small dish. A plain white tea plate or an odd plate from the charity shop works just like a large saucer.
  • A ceramic tile. An offcut of wall or floor tile costs next to nothing and is completely heatproof. Stick felt pads underneath if it might scratch the table.
  • A slate or ceramic coaster. Check the listing says it’s heat resistant.
  • A metal tray or trivet. A small tray holds several cups at once, as long as you leave a gap between them.

Don’t stand a lit candle straight on a doily, cloth, napkin, paper mat or plastic coaster. They can scorch, melt or catch light if wax spills, and a cloth makes the cup easier to pull over.

The fire safety advice for any candle is the same: put it on a heat-resistant surface, away from anything that can catch fire. Our teacup candle safety guide covers the rest.

Finding saucers and trios

Single saucers are one of the easiest things to find second-hand, because cups tend to get broken before their saucers do. Charity shops often keep them in a box or a basket and sell them for very little. Car boot sales and house clearances are good for full trios.

Online, search for the maker and pattern name from the backstamp on your cup, plus “saucer”. Our guide to Staffordshire china explains how to read a backstamp.
