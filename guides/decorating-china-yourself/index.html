Decorating china yourself: porcelain paints, pens and how to fix them

By Steven · Updated 22 November 2026

Plain white cups are among the cheapest things in any charity shop, and with a few porcelain paints or pens you can turn them into something that matches your wax, your kitchen or a person you’re making a present for. It’s also a nice way to rescue a cup with a faded or worn pattern. This guide is set out like an instructable, with the materials, a diagram for each step and notes on what lasts and what doesn’t.

DifficultyEasy
TimeAbout an hour, plus a day to dry
Makes1 decorated cup

Which paint to use

Porcelain paints are made for exactly this. The ones most people start with are water-based paints that you bake in an ordinary home oven, such as the Pebeo Porcelaine 150 range. Once baked they’re hard-wearing, and the makers say they’ll stand up to careful washing. There are glossy, matt, metallic and frosted versions.

Porcelain marker pens contain the same sort of paint in a pen. They’re much easier for lettering, dots and fine lines, and less messy than a brush. They’re baked in the same way.

Oil-based paint pens sold for crafts will mark china, but unless the pen says it’s for china and can be baked, the design is likely to scratch and wash off fairly quickly.

Ordinary permanent markers are a popular online trick, but they’re not made for china. Even after baking, the ink usually fades and washes off, so we’d only use them for something you don’t mind losing.

Decals are printed transfers. The proper ceramic ones need firing in a kiln at very high temperatures, which some pottery studios and paint-your-own-pottery cafés will do for you. The kind sold for home use without a kiln usually needs a varnish over it, which brings us to the next point.

Why not varnish

It’s tempting to paint with acrylics and seal it with varnish, but it’s not a good finish for a candle cup.

  • The cup gets warm when a candle burns, and many varnishes soften, go sticky or turn yellow with heat.
  • Varnish isn’t made for anything that touches food or lips, so it’s no good if the cup might ever be used for drinking again.
  • Varnish over glaze doesn’t grip well, so it tends to peel and scratch, especially with washing.

Baked porcelain paint avoids all of these, so it’s worth using the proper paints from the start.

Where to paint

Decorate the outside of the cup only, and keep at least 2cm below the rim clear. Most paint makers advise keeping decoration away from areas that touch lips or food, and the inside of a candle cup is where the flame and the hot wax are, so there’s no point painting there anyway. Check the label on your paint as well, because some colours, particularly the metallics, carry specific advice about food contact.

The saucer is fair game, and a little matching decoration on the saucer finishes a set nicely.

Materials

  • A china cup. Plain white or one with a small pattern is easiest.
  • Porcelain paint, porcelain marker pens or both
  • Rubbing alcohol (isopropyl alcohol) and kitchen paper

Tools

  • Fine brushes, if you’re using paint
  • Cotton buds and a little water for tidying mistakes
  • A pencil or a chinagraph pencil for marking out, if you want to plan your design
  • An ordinary kitchen oven

Step 1: Clean the cup

Wash the cup in warm soapy water and dry it well. Then wipe the outside with rubbing alcohol on kitchen paper. Fingerprints and the tiniest bit of grease stop porcelain paint from sticking properly, so try not to touch the areas you’re going to paint once they’re clean.

Step 2: Plan where the paint goes

Decide on your design before you start. Simple patterns work best on curved china: dots, small flowers, stripes round the base, a band of colour, a name or a few words. A pencil line or chinagraph pencil can mark out where things go, and it wipes off afterwards.

Keep the inside of the cup clear and leave a band of at least 2cm below the rim.

Tip: Pick a colour from your wax, or the other way round. Our colour palettes guide has ideas that work well together.

Step 3: Paint or draw the design

Shake the paint or pen well. Paint in thin coats. Thick paint can bubble or crack in the oven, so if you want a strong colour, let the first coat dry for an hour and add a second. With pens, prime them on a scrap of paper first so the paint flows evenly.

While the paint is still wet, mistakes come off with a damp cotton bud. Once it’s dry but not yet baked, you can usually scrape small slips away with a cocktail stick.

Step 4: Let it dry

Leave the cup somewhere dust-free to dry for as long as the label says before you bake it. For Porcelaine 150 this is 24 hours. Rushing this step is the most common reason paint blisters in the oven.

Step 5: Bake it

Baking is what fixes the paint and makes it hard-wearing. Follow the instructions for your paint exactly. Porcelaine 150, for example, is baked for 35 minutes at 150°C. Other brands differ, so check.

Put the cup on a baking tray in a cold oven, then turn the oven on. Starting cold means the cup warms up gradually with the oven, which is kinder to old china than putting it into a hot oven. Start timing when the oven reaches temperature.

Old gilding and the original glaze are fired at far higher temperatures than this, so a home oven won’t harm a sound cup. A cup that’s already cracked might not survive, which is one more reason to check cups carefully first. Our teacup preparation guide explains how.

Step 6: Let it cool in the oven

When the time is up, switch the oven off and leave the door closed. Let the cup cool down completely inside, which takes an hour or so. Taking hot china out into a cold kitchen is exactly the kind of sudden change that cracks old cups.

How long it lasts and how to clean it

Properly baked porcelain paint is tough, but it isn’t as hard as the original fired decoration, so treat it gently.

  • Wait a few days after baking before you wash it, as some paints carry on hardening.
  • Wash by hand in warm soapy water with a soft cloth. Even where a paint says it’s dishwasher safe, hand washing keeps it looking good for much longer.
  • Don’t use scourers or cream cleaners on the painted areas.
  • Unbaked or under-baked paint will wash off, so if a design comes away easily, it probably needed longer in the oven.

When the candle is used up, the cup can be cleaned and refilled in the usual way. See caring for old china for getting the old wax out.
