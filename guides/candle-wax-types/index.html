Candle wax types compared

Most candle waxes will work in a teacup if you get the wick right, but some are far easier than others. The main things that differ between them are how they behave as they set, how much scent they carry, how they take colour and how they look when they’re finished. Here’s what each one is like to work with.

Soy wax

Soy is the wax most people start with, and it’s what most handmade teacup candles are made from. Make sure you buy a container soy wax, because pillar grades are harder and shrink away from the sides. Container soy melts at a fairly low temperature, burns slowly and is easy to clean out of a cup with hot water.

The downsides are that it can develop white frosting on the surface, the scent is gentler than paraffin, and colours come out pastel rather than bright. None of these matter much for a teacup, where the china is the main attraction.

Rapeseed wax

Rapeseed wax is grown and made in the UK and behaves a lot like soy. It has a creamy finish and sticks well to the sides of a cup, and it’s often sold blended with soy or coconut. If you’d like a plant wax that hasn’t travelled as far, it’s worth trying, and you can usually use the same wicks and temperatures as for soy.

Coconut and coconut blends

Pure coconut wax is very soft, so it’s almost always sold as a blend with soy, rapeseed or another wax. Coconut blends set smooth and creamy with fewer surface flaws than soy, and they carry fragrance well. They cost more, but if you’re making candles to sell or give, the better finish can be worth it.

Paraffin wax

Paraffin is made during the refining of crude oil. It’s the cheapest wax, it carries scent strongly, and it takes colour better than any plant wax, giving bright, deep shades and a glossy finish. Use a grade sold for container candles.

Paraffin is more likely to soot if the wick is a little too big, and a lot of buyers now look specifically for plant waxes. For your own use it’s perfectly good, and it’s often the easiest way to get a true black or a strong red.

Beeswax

Beeswax burns slowly with a warm flame and a natural honey smell, and it has a higher melting point than the other waxes here. It doesn’t hold added fragrance very well, it’s the most expensive, and in a wide teacup it can be hard to get the melt pool to reach the edge. Pure beeswax also tends to crack or pull away from the cup as it cools.

Many makers get round this by blending beeswax with a softer wax, such as coconut or soy, which keeps the honey scent but makes the candle easier to pour and burn. If you try pure beeswax, expect to need a larger wick than you would for soy, and do a test burn first.

Blends

Blends combine the good points of two waxes. Paraffin-soy blends give brighter colour and stronger scent than soy with fewer frosting problems. Soy-coconut and rapeseed-coconut blends give a smoother top. Ready-made blends are easier than mixing your own, because the supplier will have tested the wick sizes and fragrance limits.

Gel wax

Gel wax is clear and made for glass containers, where you can see through it. In an opaque china cup there’s no real benefit, and it needs its own wicks and fragrances, so it isn’t a good choice for teacups.

Which to choose

If you’re making your first teacup candle, buy a container soy wax and follow our teacup candle instructable. Once you’re comfortable, try a coconut blend for a nicer finish, or paraffin if you want strong colours.

Whatever you choose, the wick needs to match the wax. Our guide to wax and wicks explains how to size one and read a test burn. If you have a drawer full of old candle ends, our guide to melting down old candles shows how to reuse them.
