How much wax does my teacup need?

The easiest way to know how much wax to melt is to measure how much the cup holds first. It takes two minutes, saves wasting wax, and works for any cup, mug or bowl. There are four ways to do it, and the calculator below handles all of them.

Four ways to measure a cup

Whichever you use, measure up to where you want the wax to come, which is usually about 1cm below the rim.

1. Water and a measuring jug

Fill the cup with water to the fill line, tip it into a measuring jug and read off the millilitres. It's quick and good enough for most cups, though small jugs are hard to read to within 10ml.

2. Water and kitchen scales

This is the most accurate way. Put the empty cup on the scales and press the tare button to zero them, or write down its weight. Fill it with water to the fill line and read the scales again. One gram of water is one millilitre, so the extra weight is the volume. You don't have to tip anything out, so there's nothing to spill.

With either water method, dry the cup thoroughly inside and out before you go any further. Water and wax don't mix. Even a few drops left in the bottom can spit when hot wax lands on them, and they stop the wax sticking to the china.

3. Dry rice or lentils

For crazed, cracked-glaze or fragile china that you'd rather not get wet, fill the cup with dry rice or red lentils to the fill line, level it off, then tip it into a measuring jug and read the millilitres. It's a little less accurate than water, because the grains don't settle exactly the same way twice, but it's close enough. Give the cup a wipe afterwards to get rid of any dust.

4. Measure it with a ruler

If you can't fill the cup at all, perhaps because you're planning a candle for a cup you've seen online, measure three things in centimetres: the inside width at the fill line, the inside width across the base, and the depth from the fill line down to the inside of the base. Choose "Measure with a ruler" in the calculator and it works out the volume. Teacups curve rather than having straight sides, so this is an estimate and can be out by 10% or so. Melt a little extra to be safe.

How the sum works

Wax is lighter than water, so a cup that holds 200g of water holds less than 200g of wax. The usual rule among candle makers is to multiply the water by 0.86. Waxes vary a little: supplier figures for soy run from about 0.84 to 0.90, and beeswax is a bit heavier, so we use 0.90 for that. If your supplier gives a figure for their wax, use theirs.

Fragrance oil counts towards the total, rather than going on top. So if the cup takes 172g in total and you want 8% fragrance, that's about 14g of fragrance and 158g of wax.

Tip: Melt an extra 10g to 20g. You'll want a little to top up any dip that forms around the wick as the candle sets.

Worked examples

A standard teacup. It holds 200ml up to the fill line. 200 × 0.86 = 172g. For an unscented candle, melt 172g of soy, plus a little extra for topping up.

A small coffee can with fragrance. It holds 120ml. 120 × 0.86 = about 103g in total. At 8% fragrance that's about 8g of fragrance oil and 95g of wax.

A cup measured with a ruler. It's 8cm across at the fill line, 5cm across the base and 5.5cm deep. Half of each width gives 4cm and 2.5cm. The volume is 3.14 × 5.5 ÷ 3 × (4 × 4 + 4 × 2.5 + 2.5 × 2.5), which is about 186ml. 186 × 0.86 = about 160g of wax.

A beeswax candle in a breakfast cup. It holds 300ml. 300 × 0.90 = 270g of beeswax.
